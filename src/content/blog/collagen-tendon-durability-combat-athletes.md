Fighters live and die by connective tissue. The muscle gets the attention — the protein targets, the creatine, the carbs around training — because the muscle is what the mirror shows and the scale measures. But the tissue that fails in week six of camp, the tissue that puts you out of a fight on a Tuesday mitt session three weeks before weigh-in, is not muscle. It's the tendon that holds the grip you need to pull a guillotine. It's the wrist fascia that keeps the hand aligned on a jab-cross you'll throw ten thousand times before fight night. It's the patellar tendon rebounding every time you drop under a double-leg. It's the rotator cuff holding together under eight weeks of clinch work.

Connective tissue has a different amino-acid substrate than muscle. It has a slower turnover. It has a narrower loading window for synthesis. And the standard protein recommendations in combat-sports nutrition — the 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight, delivered as whey and chicken and fish and eggs — do almost nothing for it, because the essential amino acids that build muscle are not the amino acids that build tendon.

The protocol that does build tendon is cheap, well-studied, and almost nobody in combat sports is using it right. It's 15 grams of hydrolyzed [collagen](https://scythene.com/supplements/collagen-peptides?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=collagen-tendon-durability-combat-athletes) with 50 milligrams of vitamin C, taken 30 to 60 minutes before you load the tissue. That is the Shaw-Baar protocol from the 2017 American Journal of Clinical Nutrition paper. It doubled collagen-synthesis markers in the circulation of the subjects who dosed before intermittent jumping exercise. It is one of the few supplement protocols where the mechanism, the dose, the timing, and the output marker all line up in the same direction, in a controlled trial, in a human population doing the kind of loading that applies to combat sports.

Here is how to actually run it across a fight camp.

## Why Collagen Specifically

Tendon, ligament, cartilage, and fascia are built from type I and type III collagen. The amino acids they rebuild with — glycine, proline, hydroxyproline, lysine — are abundant in collagen and sparse in whey, chicken, and eggs. Hydroxyproline in particular is a non-essential amino acid that is essentially unique to collagen. Your body makes it by post-translationally hydroxylating proline in the collagen peptide, and the enzyme that does that — prolyl hydroxylase — requires vitamin C as a cofactor. That is why the Shaw-Baar protocol pairs collagen with vitamin C. It is not a loose antioxidant pairing. It is substrate plus required cofactor.

The second piece is bioavailability. Hydrolyzed collagen peptides are small enough — 2 to 5 kilodaltons — to be absorbed intact in meaningful quantities. The specific peptides proline-hydroxyproline and glycine-proline-hydroxyproline show up in human plasma within 60 to 90 minutes of ingestion, and they are the signal that drives the local fibroblast to upregulate collagen synthesis at a loaded tissue. Gelatin, which is partially hydrolyzed collagen, works too — Shaw's original protocol used gelatin — but it is harder to mix pre-training and the hydrolyzed-peptide product is now cheaper per gram at competitive-grade brands.

Whey protein does not do this. Casein does not do this. A chicken breast at your pre-training meal does not do this. The protein that rebuilds the tendon has to be collagen or gelatin, delivered in the loading window.

## The 60-Minute Window

The reason timing matters: the pulse of circulating collagen-peptide signal from ingestion peaks around 60 minutes post-dose and lasts roughly two hours. Mechanical loading of a tendon or ligament triggers the local fibroblast to pull substrate out of circulation and lay it down into new collagen fibrils in the hours that follow. If the circulating substrate is already elevated when the loading hits — meaning you dosed 30 to 60 minutes before training — the synthesis signal gets amplified. If you dose after training, the window has largely closed by the time the peptides are in circulation.

That is the single most common mistake with this protocol. Fighters who have read about collagen take it with their post-training protein shake, treat it like muscle protein, and get a fraction of the tendon-synthesis effect. The dose has to be in before the loading session, not after.

For combat-sports application: 30 to 60 minutes before gi training, before bag work, before sparring, before plyometric or lifting work that loads the tendon-heavy structures. If you have a double session, dose before the one that loads the tissue you care about most — grappling-side for the grappler, striking-side for the striker.

## The Combat-Athlete Loading Map

**Grapplers.** The highest-ROI loading surface is the hand. The A2 and A4 pulleys of the fingers take the full load of a gi grip, a wrist grip under a kimura, a cross-collar choke setup, and the thirty-plus minute drilling blocks that stack those loads. The DIP and PIP joints compress under grip retention. The wrist flexors and extensors load under the thousand-plus grip exchanges per training week. Pulley injuries in BJJ are so common that the climbing-literature grading system (grade I through grade IV pulley rupture) is now standard in BJJ sports-medicine clinics. Dose collagen 45 minutes before gi training, three to five times per week during camp.

**Strikers.** The wrist is the structure that fails. Not the knuckle itself, in most cases, but the carpal-tunnel-adjacent soft tissue and the extensor retinaculum that holds the hand aligned on impact. Eight to twelve weeks of mitt-plus-bag-plus-sparring at ten thousand strikes per week accumulates loading on tissue that recovers at a tenth of the rate of muscle. Collagen in the pre-bag-session window, with hand wraps on and gloves ready, is the protocol. The data on this specific application is thinner than the grappler-grip data, but the mechanism is the same — connective tissue, loaded, in the window.

**Everyone.** The patellar tendon rebounds every rebound in a shadow-box session, every penetration step on a double-leg, every explosive sprawl. The Achilles loads on every footwork drill. The rotator cuff compresses under eight weeks of clinch and shoulder-pin positions. The hip-adductor-insertion tendon absorbs guard retention and underhook work. The hip-flexor insertion takes the brunt of a sprawl. Across a camp, the whole tendon system gets loaded, and most fighters have one or two specific sites that chronically irritate — the fighter's own injury history is the map of what to protect.

## The 8-Week Camp Protocol

**Weeks 1 to 4 (base block).** 10 grams of hydrolyzed collagen plus 100 milligrams of vitamin C, dosed 45 to 60 minutes before the day's primary loading session. If you train twice, dose before the session that loads your highest-risk tissue. Mix into water or a pre-training carb drink. Separate dose from the pre-training coffee — caffeine is not an issue, but you want the collagen in a clean fluid rather than displacing an eating window.

**Weeks 5 to 8 (build and peak).** 15 grams of hydrolyzed collagen plus 100 to 200 milligrams of vitamin C, 45 to 60 minutes before the primary session, five to six days per week. On double-session days, add a 10-gram dose before the second session if the second session loads tendon-heavy structures (sparring, grappling live rolls, plyometrics). Total daily can run to 20 to 25 grams across the week-5-to-week-8 block without any observed downside in the literature.

**Fight week (final 7 days).** Drop to a maintenance 10 grams per day at breakfast. The training load has tapered, the loading-window benefit is smaller, and you don't want to be mixing powders in the hotel room on fight night. The tissue work you can do in fight week is already banked.

**Recovery/injured days.** If you are off the mat with a tendon-side injury — a strained adductor, an inflamed patellar tendon, a cranky wrist — 10 grams twice per day with vitamin C, taken 60 minutes before the two rehab sessions that load the injured tissue (a prehab block, a slow-tempo isometric block). The data on recovery-side acceleration is less robust than the prevention-side data, but the downside is zero and the cost is low.

## Pairing With the Rest of the Stack

Collagen does not replace your protein target. Twenty grams of collagen does not count as twenty grams of protein toward your 1.8-grams-per-kilogram target, because collagen is low in the essential amino acids (leucine, in particular) that drive muscle-protein synthesis. Collagen is additive to the whey/casein/chicken/beef base, not a substitute for it. The fighter who swaps a whey shake for a collagen scoop at breakfast is losing muscle-protein-synthesis signaling to gain tendon-synthesis signaling. Don't swap. Add.

Omega-3 EPA plus DHA at a separate meal supports the broader inflammatory recovery around high-load training and does not compete with the collagen loading window. Magnesium at night supports sleep and does not interact. Creatine is unrelated and runs on its own schedule.

Vitamin C is the one supplement that pairs directly. 50 to 200 milligrams in the same dose. More than that doesn't improve the collagen-synthesis effect in the published data, and the fat-and-water-soluble vitamin behavior of C makes the oversized dose a wash by urinary excretion.

## The Four Mistakes That Gut the Protocol

**Mistake one: dosing after training.** The window is pre-loading, not post. The post-training dose is a muscle-protein-synthesis timing frame, which is a different protocol. Move the collagen to 30 to 60 minutes before the session.

**Mistake two: using collagen as primary protein.** Twenty grams of collagen is not twenty grams of complete protein. Keep the whey, chicken, fish, and eggs intact as the muscle-protein base. Add collagen on top of that total, not instead of some of it.

**Mistake three: relying on bone broth for the dose.** Bone broth is a fine food. It is also unreliable in collagen content — a cup can deliver anywhere from 2 to 10 grams depending on preparation, bones, and simmer time. For a protocol that needs a defined dose in a defined window, use hydrolyzed collagen powder with a labeled per-serving gram count. Food-based broth is a nice-to-have on top of the protocol, not the protocol itself.

**Mistake four: skipping the vitamin C.** The prolyl hydroxylase enzyme that builds hydroxyproline requires vitamin C as a cofactor. If your diet is heavy in citrus, bell pepper, kiwi, strawberries, or broccoli, you may clear the cofactor requirement from food alone, but the protocol as published pairs 50 to 200 milligrams of C directly with the collagen dose. The cost is pennies. Include it.

## The Verdict

Collagen is tier-2 for muscle protein synthesis, which is why most sports-nutrition guidance treats it as a secondary supplement. For tendon, ligament, cartilage, and fascia — the tissues that chronically break in fight camps and knock fighters off cards — it is tier-1. It is one of the few supplements where the mechanism, the dose, the timing, and the loading application line up cleanly in the published trial data, and where the combat-sports loading pattern (grip, wrist, patellar, cuff) matches the tissues the protocol specifically protects.

The protocol: 10 to 15 grams of hydrolyzed collagen plus 50 to 200 milligrams of vitamin C, 30 to 60 minutes before the day's primary loading session, five to six days per week across camp. Added to, not swapped for, your muscle-protein base. Dropped to maintenance in fight week. Carried through to recovery blocks when the tissue is injured.
