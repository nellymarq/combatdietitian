It is Wednesday afternoon. The flyweight is 7 lbs over the 125-lb mark. Weigh-in is Friday at 9 a.m. The coach is running the sauna rotation. The fighter is in the shower, has been in it for 20 minutes, and when he came out of the sauna for the second round he was walking like a drunk and his voice was off a half-step. The cornerman tells you he threw up water on the walk from the hotel. The strength and conditioning coach says to push through — "he always finds a way."

Someone in that room has to make the call. In most camps that person is the dietitian or the cornerman. The coach is paid to get the fighter to the scale. The fighter is incentivized to make weight even if his body is telling him to stop. The commission doctor is not in the room until the official weigh-in. The person who is in the room and can read the signs is you.

Pulling a fighter mid-cut costs a purse, costs a title shot, costs a sponsor relationship, costs reputation. Not pulling a fighter who should have been pulled costs the fighter. Sometimes it costs a career, sometimes it costs a kidney, and in rare cases — the 2013 Leandro Souza case out of Brazil, the Yang Jian Bing cut in 2015, the handful of under-the-radar amateur deaths that never made international press — it costs a life.

The right call is almost always the earlier call. The red flags below are the clinical findings that end the discussion. If any one of these shows up in the final 72 hours, the cut is over. The fighter either weighs in heavier, moves the fight, or does not fight at all. This is not an opinion. This is the floor.

## Red Flag 1 — Resting heart rate at baseline plus 25 bpm, or above 110 at rest

Heart rate climbs when the body is defending blood pressure against falling plasma volume. The sympathetic nervous system is dumping catecholamines to maintain cardiac output against a shrinking circulating volume. The fighter's baseline morning resting heart rate across the camp is the comparator — most elite combat athletes sit between 42 and 55 bpm. A sustained resting heart rate of baseline + 25, or an absolute resting heart rate above 110 bpm in the final 24 to 72 hours of a cut, is a flag that the volume deficit is beyond what passive diuresis can account for. The fighter is in or approaching compensated hypovolemic shock.

**The threshold:** resting HR above 110 bpm, or baseline + 25 bpm, sustained for more than one measurement 15 minutes apart.

**What to do:** stop active dehydration (no sauna, no suit work, no further water deprivation). Oral rehydration begins immediately with an electrolyte solution. If HR is above 120 at rest with any other red flag on this list present, call the fight medic.

## Red Flag 2 — Systolic blood pressure drop above 15 mmHg on standing

Orthostatic blood pressure is the cheapest, fastest intravascular-volume check in the room. Lay the fighter supine for 5 minutes, take a BP reading, stand them up for 1 minute, take another reading. In a well-hydrated athlete the systolic number moves by less than 10 mmHg. A drop of 15 mmHg or more flags clinically meaningful intravascular depletion. A drop above 20 mmHg with symptoms (dizziness, visual grey-out, feeling like they are going to pass out when they stand) is a flag the fighter should not be upright, let alone cutting.

**The threshold:** systolic drop above 15 mmHg standing from supine, or above 10 mmHg with any orthostatic symptom.

**What to do:** stop the cut. Supine rest. Oral rehydration. If the fighter cannot tolerate standing without symptoms after 60 minutes of oral intake, escalate to the fight medic — this is where an IV becomes a medical decision, not a performance decision, and the commission rules on IV use after that point are the medic's problem, not yours.

## Red Flag 3 — Weight loss rate above 1.5 percent body mass per 24 hours in the final 72 hours

The physiology of water weight has a ceiling. Even a well-run sauna-plus-suit sequence should not pull more than 1 to 1.2 percent of body mass per 24 hours in the final cut window if the fighter is already in the glycogen-and-gut-content-depleted state. A rate above 1.5 percent per 24 hours means one of two things is happening: the fighter is losing lean mass and extracellular water simultaneously (metabolically dangerous), or there is a measurement error on the scale that is masking an actual plateau and leading the coach to push harder. Either answer is a stop sign.

**The threshold:** more than 1.5 percent body mass loss in any single 24-hour window inside the final 72 hours, measured on a known-calibrated scale at the same time of day each day.

**What to do:** pause the cut. Rebaseline with a fresh scale reading at a different time of day. Rehydrate orally. If the rate is confirmed above 1.5 percent per 24 hours after a 4-hour pause with 500 mL of electrolyte fluid, the cut is over.

## Red Flag 4 — Urine output below 500 mL in 24 hours, or dark coffee-colored urine

Normal urine output in an active adult is 800 to 2000 mL per 24 hours. The kidneys reduce output under water restriction, and some reduction is expected during a cut. Below 500 mL per 24 hours is the oliguria threshold at which the renal tubules are concentrating urine beyond their safe operating range and the risk of acute tubular necrosis climbs. The darker-than-black-tea or coffee-colored urine is the myoglobinuria signal — rhabdomyolysis is in progress, muscle protein is being filtered through the kidneys, and the risk of acute kidney injury is immediate.

**The threshold:** below 500 mL urine output in 24 hours, or any single void with coffee-colored urine, or urine that stains the toilet bowl brown.

**What to do:** coffee-colored urine ends the cut. Full stop. The fighter goes to the hospital for a CK (creatine kinase) draw and renal function panel. CK above 5000 IU/L with any symptom (muscle pain, weakness, dark urine) is rhabdomyolysis and the fighter is not fighting this weekend. The oliguria without discoloration is a stop-the-cut-and-rehydrate situation that may resolve in 4 to 6 hours with 1 to 1.5 liters of oral electrolyte intake — if it does not, escalate.

## Red Flag 5 — Mental status change, slurred speech, confusion, or inability to answer orientation questions

The brain tolerates hypovolemia and electrolyte derangement poorly. A fighter who cannot tell you the day of the week, who is slurring words, who answers slowly to simple questions, who seems "foggy" or "spacey" past what the dehydration-and-cut context would predict, has crossed a line. The electrolyte-and-volume state is affecting the central nervous system. The next step down is a seizure or a syncopal event.

**The threshold:** any failure of a basic orientation check (name, place, day, who the opponent is, what time weigh-in is), slurred or slow speech that is new, any new unsteady gait or coordination problem, or a fighter who seems to drift out of the conversation. The sauna hallucinations some fighters describe at the end of a hard cut sit in this red-flag category — "funny but normal for me" is not an acceptable framing.

**What to do:** stop the cut. Cold water or ice to the groin and neck to drop core temperature if the sauna was in progress. Oral rehydration. Call the fight medic. A mental status change in combination with any other red flag on this list is an emergency room visit, not a cornerman consultation.

## Red Flag 6 — Core body temperature above 38.5 degrees Celsius during cut activity

Elevated core temperature during a sauna session or suit work is the point of the exercise and is not a red flag in itself. The red flag is a core temperature that is still above 38.5 C 30 minutes after the fighter has exited the heat and begun the cool-down. A sustained hyperthermia above that threshold outside the heat environment means the body's cooling mechanisms are failing — the plasma volume is low enough that cutaneous blood flow for radiative cooling is compromised, sweat rate has dropped (or stopped), and the fighter is on the slope toward heat stroke.

**The threshold:** rectal or ear-canal temperature above 38.5 C sustained 30 minutes after exiting the heat environment, or any temperature above 39.5 C at any time.

**What to do:** aggressive cooling. Ice packs to the groin, axillae, and neck. Immersion if available (a bathtub with ice and water is the gold standard). The sauna sessions are over for the camp — this fighter does not go back in. Call the fight medic. Hyperthermia at this level with any mental status change is an emergency room transport.

## Red Flag 7 — Severe muscle cramping with CK draw above 5000 IU/L or myoglobinuria

Most hard cuts produce some muscle cramping in the final 24 to 48 hours — the mixed electrolyte-and-magnesium deficit against the dehydrated state is the cause, and the cramps usually resolve with mineral replacement and a few hours of oral rehydration. The red-flag cramping is different: it is severe, it does not resolve with the usual electrolyte intake, it is accompanied by muscle pain at rest, and it coincides with any of the muscle-damage signals — the coffee-colored urine of Red Flag 4, significant muscle weakness beyond cut fatigue, or a CK draw if the camp has access to point-of-care labs.

**The threshold:** cramping that is bilateral and does not resolve with 1 g sodium plus 400 mg magnesium intake over 60 minutes, resting muscle pain in the quads or back or shoulders out of proportion to activity, or a CK level above 5000 IU/L (point-of-care devices like the i-STAT run this; a standard commercial lab in-camp will not turn it around in the cut window).

**What to do:** this one is a stop-and-escalate flag regardless of the CK number. The combination of severe cramping and any other red flag on this list is enough. The fighter is not making weight at the cost of his kidneys.

## The decision chain — how to escalate

The red-flag findings above do not require all seven to be present. Any single one of them is enough to pull the fighter. Two or more in combination is an emergency escalation, not a judgment call.

**Who makes the call:** the dietitian, the cornerman, and the head coach jointly, with the dietitian or the cornerman having a veto. If the head coach is pushing to continue past a red flag, the veto is the dietitian's responsibility to exercise. The coach is not the medic. The dietitian or the cornerman standing between the fighter and a lost kidney is doing the job.

**How to break the news to the fighter:** the frame is "we are not making this cut safely" rather than "you failed." The fighter is often the last person in the room to want to stop — the sunk-cost of 8 weeks of camp, the money on the line, the dream of the title shot, all argue against stopping. The dietitian's job is to be the voice that stops it. The fighter's job is to listen.

**The next-week conversation:** after the fighter has rehydrated and recovered, the conversation is about whether this weight class is sustainable. A fighter who hits red flags on this cut will hit them on the next one too, and the next one after that, until he moves up. Red flags on consecutive cuts in the same weight class is the signal that it is time to change weight class permanently.

## The three measurements every camp should have on the counter

If your camp does not already have these three tools in the weight-cut kit, add them before the next cut:

**A finger pulse oximeter with a heart-rate readout.** $20 at any pharmacy. Reads resting heart rate in 10 seconds. Covers Red Flag 1.

**A manual or automatic blood pressure cuff.** $30 to $60. Reads supine and standing BP for the orthostatic check. Covers Red Flag 2.

**A calibrated bathroom scale that reads to 0.1 lb or 0.05 kg.** $40 to $100. Reads consistent weight at the same time each day. Covers Red Flag 3.

The three tools cost less than a single fight-week supplement order. A camp that cannot afford them is a camp that cannot afford to cut safely.

## What the dietitian carries into the room

Beyond the three tools, the dietitian walking into a cut should bring: an electrolyte supply the fighter has rehearsed with across camp (a mixed sodium-potassium-magnesium formulation at 1000 to 1500 mg sodium per liter is the baseline for the rehydration window), a magnesium bisglycinate supply for cramping management, a thermometer (ear-canal or rectal, not a forehead-strip), and the fight medic's cell phone number saved and tested before Fight Week.

The point of the kit is not to push the cut further. The point of the kit is to catch the red flags earlier, treat the fixable ones (mild cramping, mild dehydration, mild electrolyte derangement) before they become stop-the-cut findings, and to recognize the stop-the-cut findings when they appear.

## The honest closing line

Every camp I have worked with has a fighter in it who should not be at his current weight class. The fighter knows it. The coach knows it. The dietitian knows it. The fight is scheduled, the money is signed, the walk-up is set, and the cut proceeds anyway. The red-flag framework is the structure that gives the camp a reason to stop a cut that should be stopped — a numeric, defensible, non-negotiable floor that overrides the pressure to keep going.

If you catch a red flag early enough, you lose a fight. If you do not catch it at all, you lose a fighter. The job is to catch it early.
