The scale, the pace of the cut, the sodium board, the carb refeed, the rehydration curve — those are the numbers coaches and nutritionists chase. Teeth are not on the list. The teeth come up when a fighter three camps into a career looks in the mirror and notices the edges of the incisors have gone translucent, the molars feel sharp against the tongue in places they didn't before, and a dentist's visit confirms that the enamel has worn through to the dentin on the lingual surfaces of the top front teeth. The question that follows is always the same: when did this happen? And the honest answer is almost always: it happened on fight week.

Enamel is the hardest tissue in the human body. It is also the one tissue that does not regenerate. Once the hydroxyapatite crystal matrix dissolves, the body does not rebuild it. Dentin can be covered, cavities can be filled, gums can be grafted, bone can be rebuilt around an implant. Enamel is gone forever. And three of the single most erosive exposures in sports — stomach acid from vomiting, acid reflux from sleeping on an empty stomach, and acidic sports drinks sipped over hours — all land in the 72 hours between the last meal of the water-cut and the first full meal post-fight. The cumulative dose across a six-fight-per-year career is enough to produce dental erosion patterns that bariatric-surgery clinics and bulimia-recovery clinics recognize on sight.

This is the fight-week dental-health protocol. It costs nothing. It takes 90 seconds twice a day on fight week. It will not change the cut, the rehydration curve, or the fight-day fueling. And it is the one piece of the camp almost no one has written down.

## The Three Erosive Exposures and Why They Stack on Fight Week

**Stomach acid from forced vomiting.** The water-and-food cut in the final 48 to 72 hours empties the stomach while the sauna and sweat protocols drop body weight. Fighters at the deepest end of the cut — the 10-plus-percent-body-weight cutters, the fighters sitting in a hot bath Friday night to shed the last pound — frequently vomit, either from the sauna, from the dehydration-driven nausea, or from the forced attempts to purge any residual food or water in the stomach. Gastric contents sit at pH 1.5 to 3.5. Enamel begins to demineralize at pH 5.5. Every vomiting episode floods the oral cavity, particularly the lingual (tongue-side) surfaces of the upper front teeth and the occlusal surfaces of the lower molars, with a chemical bath 10,000 times more acidic than the demineralization threshold. The characteristic bulimia-erosion pattern — the "perimolysis" the dental literature named after the Greek for "around the molar" — is a clinical signature every general dentist is trained to spot, and it accumulates on the fighter the same way it accumulates on the patient with an active eating disorder.

**Acid reflux from the empty stomach.** The second exposure is quieter but equally damaging. The fighter who stops eating 48 hours out and stops drinking 24 hours out spends two nights sleeping on an empty stomach. The lower esophageal sphincter relaxes during sleep. The gastric contents — now acid-only, no food buffer — reflux into the esophagus and, in a non-trivial fraction of fighters, up into the oropharynx and the mouth. The fighter wakes with a sour taste, a scratchy throat, and no memory of a specific event. The reflux happened in sleep, deposited pH-2 gastric acid on the back of the throat and the lingual surfaces of the upper teeth, and sat there against the enamel until the morning. The chronic sleep-reflux pattern across repeated camps is a documented driver of dental erosion in the gastroenterology literature. The fighter population is not usually screened for it.

**Citric-acid-plus-sugar sports drinks over the rehydration window.** The post-weigh-in rehydration load — the Pedialyte, the sports drinks, the carb-dense fluid refuel over 18 to 24 hours — is where the third exposure lands. Most commercial sports drinks are acidic by design. Citric acid gives them their shelf life and their bright flavor, and it drops the pH of the drink to between 2.4 and 3.5 depending on the brand. Gatorade is around 2.9. Powerade is around 2.8. Pedialyte is around 4.0, better but still below the demineralization threshold. A fighter sipping 2 liters of sports drink across the first six hours post-weigh-in to rebuild carbohydrate and electrolyte stores is bathing the enamel in an acidic solution continuously across that window. The sipping pattern — small sips, slow volume, over hours — is worse than drinking the same total volume quickly. The sip pattern never lets the saliva neutralize the mouth back to baseline pH. The enamel sits sub-5.5 for the entire window.

Stack the three. Vomiting on Thursday and Friday from the water cut. Reflux across both nights of empty-stomach sleep. Six-plus hours of continuous sports-drink exposure Saturday morning. That is fight week.

## Why Fighters Are the Highest-Risk Athletic Population

Endurance athletes drink sports drinks, but they do it over single events of two to five hours, not consecutive weeks of camp into a weigh-in cut. Bariatric patients get the vomiting exposure but under medical supervision with a dentist in the loop from day one. Bulimia patients get the vomiting exposure chronically but are typically identified by a general dentist on the three-month cleaning. The fighter gets all three exposures simultaneously, repeats the cycle six to eight times a year across a career, and is almost never asked about dental health by anybody in the corner.

The timing is also uniquely bad. Enamel is softest in the 30 to 60 minutes after an acid exposure. The dentin-protective layer of saliva — the one that remineralizes the surface across the normal day — is suppressed in the dehydrated fighter sitting at 10 percent below walkaround weight. Saliva flow drops in dehydration. Buffer capacity drops. The pH-neutralization window that would normally close in 20 minutes after a cola or an orange juice stretches to an hour or more in the dehydrated state. The enamel is naked against the acid for longer than it would be in a normally hydrated person taking the same exposure. The fight week exposure is both more frequent and worse-timed against the body's natural defense than any civilian equivalent.

## The 90-Second Fight-Week Protocol

**Do not brush within 30 minutes of a vomiting episode, a reflux event, or a sports-drink sip.** This is the first rule and it is the one most fighters get wrong. Freshly acid-exposed enamel is soft. Brushing softened enamel mechanically removes the demineralized surface layer and accelerates the damage. The instinct after vomiting is to brush immediately. The correct action is to rinse with water or a baking-soda solution first, wait 30 to 60 minutes for the saliva to re-mineralize the surface, and then brush.

**Rinse with sodium bicarbonate solution after every acid exposure.** One teaspoon (about 5 grams) of baking soda in 8 ounces of water, swish for 30 seconds, spit. Do not swallow — the sodium load during a water cut is already managed and an extra 1,200 milligrams of sodium from a swallowed bicarbonate rinse will throw the board off. Baking soda raises the oral pH immediately into the mid-8s, well above the demineralization threshold, and gives the enamel a 20-to-30-minute buffer window to re-harden before any brushing. Keep a jar of baking soda next to the bathroom sink the entire fight week. Rinse after every vomit episode, every reflux event, every suspected acid exposure, and every sports-drink interval.

**Switch the water bottle on fight day from pure sports drink to a sports-drink-and-water alternation.** Sip sports drink for 15 minutes, then switch to plain water for 15 minutes, then back. The alternation lets the saliva neutralize the mouth back toward neutral pH for a third of the rehydration window instead of leaving the enamel in an acid bath for six continuous hours. The total carbohydrate and electrolyte delivery does not change — the volume redistributes across the window. Pedialyte over Gatorade for the same reason: higher pH, less erosive at the same volume. If a specific flavor of sports drink is the one the fighter likes, dilute it 2:1 with water in the first six hours post-weigh-in and the pH climbs toward 3.5 to 4.0 from the native 2.8 to 2.9.

**Use a straw for all acidic beverages the entire fight week.** A straw placed behind the front teeth delivers the drink to the back of the mouth, bypasses the front-tooth lingual surfaces that take the heaviest erosion load, and reduces the total enamel contact time per sip. This is a civilian recommendation for anyone who drinks soda or juice regularly and it applies verbatim to the fighter on fight week. Keep a pack of flexible straws in the hotel bag.

**Fluoride-rinse the night before weigh-in and the night of the fight.** A standard 0.05-percent sodium-fluoride rinse (available over the counter — Act or any store-brand equivalent) remineralizes the enamel across the overnight window and strengthens the hydroxyapatite crystal against the next exposure. Fifteen milliliters, swish for 60 seconds, spit, no eating or drinking for 30 minutes. The two highest-value nights to use it are the night before weigh-in (buffer against the next-day vomit and reflux exposure) and the night of the fight (recovery against the sports-drink load).

**Do not use whitening toothpaste during fight week.** Whitening pastes contain abrasives — hydrated silica, calcium carbonate, bentonite clay — that mechanically polish the enamel surface. On fight week, the enamel is softer than usual from the repeated acid exposure, and the abrasive polish will remove more surface than it would on a normal week. Switch to a sensitivity toothpaste (Sensodyne or equivalent) with potassium nitrate and stannous fluoride for the fight-week block. These pastes are formulated for exactly this kind of exposure.

## The Chronic Fighter and the Dental-Exam Cadence

The protocol above handles the acute fight-week exposure. The chronic fighter — the one who has been through a dozen camps already, the one who already shows erosion at the dentist — needs the longitudinal piece.

Twice-yearly dental cleanings are not enough for the active fighting population. The cadence should be a full exam plus cleaning every three months during the active fighting phase of the career, aligned so one of the four appointments lands in the two weeks after each fight. The two-week post-fight window is when the dentist can read the acute damage from the last camp and apply fluoride varnish or remineralizing agents (CPP-ACP paste, Recaldent, prescription-strength neutral sodium fluoride varnish) to the surfaces that took the heaviest hit before the next camp starts. The post-fight varnish is a 10-minute appointment and it is the single highest-value dental intervention a fighter can run.

Tell the dentist you are an active fighter. Tell them specifically about the water cut, the sauna, the vomiting pattern, the sports-drink rehydration, and the reflux history. Most general dentists do not know what fight camp looks like and will treat the erosion pattern as idiopathic. The patient-history detail changes the treatment plan.

Night guards, if the fighter already uses one for sparring-related jaw clenching, should be remade every 18 to 24 months during the active fighting phase. The guard sits against the enamel through the night and, in a reflux-prone fighter, becomes a reservoir for the refluxed acid. A guard worn for three years without replacement accumulates acid residue and biofilm that compounds the erosion. Guards also wear down against the opposing teeth and lose their protective bite registration over camps that involve heavy jaw clenching under weight-cut stress.

## The Pre-Weigh-In Checklist for the Corner

The corner can add dental protection to the standard fight-week checklist without changing anything else. Baking soda in a small jar next to the hotel sink and the sauna bathroom. Fluoride mouth rinse in the kit bag. Flexible straws for the water bottles and the sports drinks. Sensitivity toothpaste as the fight-week paste. Note on the fighter's intake form of the last dental exam date and any flagged erosion sites. Reminder, after any sauna-related vomiting episode, to rinse with baking soda first, wait, brush later.

The whole protocol runs on 90 seconds and a $4 box of baking soda. The fighter will not notice it in the mirror the next day. They will notice it fifteen years into the career when the front teeth still look like front teeth and the dentist is not asking why the lingual surfaces have gone translucent.

The nutrition plan protects the fight. The dental protocol protects the career after it.
