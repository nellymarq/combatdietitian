The phone rings on Monday afternoon. A fighter on Saturday-two-weeks-out's card blew out an ACL in the last sparring session, the promoter is calling every manager in the region, and your fighter is on the list because his walking-around weight is close enough to the vacant slot and the promoter knows he trains hard year-round. The purse is real, the win bonus is real, the exposure is real, and your fighter wants the fight.

You have 14 days to weigh in.

The instinct in most gyms is to say yes on Monday afternoon and figure out the plan Tuesday morning. That instinct is how short-notice fighters show up to weigh-ins looking hollow-eyed at their announced weight, gas in round one from a compressed cut and a fight camp that never happened, and either lose the fight or win it in a way that gives them nothing to build on for the next one. Short notice is not just a compressed 8-week camp. It is a different problem with a different playbook, and the first move is a hard-eyed inventory that decides whether the fight is takeable at all before anyone signs the contract.

Here is the 14-day short-notice protocol — the pre-notice inventory that decides whether you can safely take the fight, the weight-differential math that determines the cut plan, the day-by-day training-and-nutrition schedule, the supplement stack that has time to work in a 14-day window, the fight-week and fight-day plan, and the honest read on when to turn the fight down.

## Before you sign: the 90-minute inventory

Do not agree to a short-notice fight before you have run this inventory. The five questions that decide whether the fight is takeable:

**How far off contract weight are you today?** Weigh in on your gym scale, right now, in training gear. Compare against the announced weight for the vacant slot. The differential — call it Δ — is the single most important number in the decision.

- Δ ≤ 3% of body mass (≈4-5 lb at 145, 5-7 lb at 170): fully takeable. This is a fight-week rehydration cut plus a small nutrition-side drop across the two weeks. No aggressive water manipulation needed until the final 72 hours.
- Δ 3-6% (≈7-9 lb at 145, 10-13 lb at 170): takeable with a real cut. Structured nutrition-side drop across 10 days plus a full fight-week water-and-carb protocol at the end. Uncomfortable but standard fight-camp territory.
- Δ 6-9% (≈10-13 lb at 145, 15-19 lb at 170): borderline. Requires an aggressive nutrition-side drop from Day 14 and a full sauna-optional water cut at Day 3. Every mistake in Week 1 costs you fight-night sharpness.
- Δ > 9%: turn it down or negotiate a catch weight. Attempting a 10%+ cut on 14 days notice is the pathway to failed weigh-in, DKD (dangerous kidney damage) or ER referrals, gassing catastrophically, and finishing careers early.

**Are you in shape?** Not "have you been training" — actually in shape. Sparring within the last 3 weeks, conditioning sessions within the last 10 days, no lingering injuries. If your last hard round was six weeks ago and you have been base-building, you are not fit enough to take a fight on 14 days regardless of the weight math.

**What is your current glycogen and hydration status?** If you are three days out from your last hard training block, walking around depleted from a bad food-log week, or in a caloric deficit for a body-composition goal, your Day 1 starting point is 3-5 pounds below your true walking weight and your cut math is already wrong.

**What are your current biomarkers?** If you have panels from the last 4 to 6 weeks — hemoglobin, ferritin, testosterone in male fighters, menstrual-cycle status in female fighters — factor them in. A ferritin of 22 and a hemoglobin at the bottom of the reference range means you have less physiologic reserve to spend on an aggressive cut and less capacity to bounce back for the fight itself.

**Are you healthy this week?** Any active URI, GI distress, poor sleep for the last week, or elevated resting heart rate is a signal that the immune system and the nervous system are already partially spent. Adding a short-notice cut on top of a partially-compromised system is asking for a Week 1 collapse.

If any of those five answers reads "no" or "borderline" and the weight differential is above 6%, the responsible answer is either a catch-weight negotiation or a decline. The purse is not worth a hospitalization or a career-shortening chronic-cut pattern. See [post-weigh-in mistakes](/blog/post-weigh-in-mistakes-24-hours-that-lose-fights) for how bad cuts show up on fight night even when the scale reads clean.

## The weight-differential math

Once you have Δ, here is the math that structures the cut plan across 14 days.

The Day 14-to-Day 3 window is nutrition-side. It moves body composition (fat loss, glycogen manipulation) and gut content. Sustainable nutrition-side drop across 11 days is 1.0 to 1.5% of body mass per week under aggressive but sane conditions — call it 1.5 to 3 lb at 145, 2 to 4 lb at 170, across the full 11-day window. This is the fat-loss-plus-modest-glycogen-tapering component.

The Day 3-to-Day 1 window is water. Aggressive water manipulation across 72 hours can pull 3 to 6% of body mass depending on baseline hydration status, sauna access, and how well the fighter tolerates it. This is the classical fight-week water cut.

The Day 1-to-weigh-in window is the final overnight — additional 1 to 2% from an overnight sauna suit session, morning bathroom, and terminal dehydration in the hours before the scale.

Sum of the three windows: 5.5 to 10% of body mass across 14 days in the aggressive-but-defensible range. Anything above that pushes past what the physiology supports on this timeline.

If your Δ is 5%, the cut plan is: 1.5% nutrition-side across Day 14 to Day 3, 3% water across Day 3 to Day 1, 0.5% overnight. Comfortable.

If your Δ is 8%, the plan is: 2.5% nutrition-side across Day 14 to Day 3, 4.5% water across Day 3 to Day 1, 1% overnight. Uncomfortable but doable for a fighter with the reserve.

If your Δ is above 9%, the numbers do not fit inside the safe-and-effective envelope on this timeline. Negotiate a catch weight or decline.

See [weight-cut science](/blog/weight-cut-science) for the physiology behind these numbers and [same-day vs day-before weigh-in protocols](/blog/same-day-vs-day-before-weigh-in-protocols) for the format-specific overlay.

## Day 14 to Day 8: nutrition-side load

This is Week 1 of the short-notice camp. The goal is threefold: preserve conditioning without adding new fatigue, drop 1.5 to 3 lb of nutrition-side weight, and top off every recoverable capacity — glycogen, mineral status, sleep debt, immune reserve — so the fighter walks into Week 2 with the best possible substrate for the aggressive final phase.

**Training.** Two hard sessions across Week 1, maximum. One sparring session at 70 to 80% intensity in the middle of the week (Wednesday if the fight is on the second Saturday) to keep timing and reaction speed sharp without the immune and CNS cost of a full sparring day. One conditioning session (Sunday or Tuesday) at a moderate-to-hard effort — a 20-minute assault-bike interval block, a 40-minute steady-state run, or a metcon block scaled to 75%. Everything else is technique work, positional drilling, mitts, mobility, and light lifting at 60% intensity.

The tempting error in Week 1 of a short-notice camp is to try to build fitness. You cannot build fitness in 14 days. You can only preserve what you have and shed what will slow you down. Every hard session in Week 1 that goes past the 80% intensity ceiling costs Week 2 sharpness at a poor exchange rate.

**Nutrition.** Caloric intake in a modest deficit — 300 to 500 kcal/day below maintenance, calibrated to the fighter's normal training-day calorie count. Protein at 2.0 to 2.4 g/kg body mass to preserve lean mass through the deficit. Carbohydrate at 4 to 5 g/kg on hard-training days and 3 to 4 g/kg on lighter days — enough to fuel the sessions and keep glycogen topped, not so much that fat loss stalls. Fat at 0.8 to 1.0 g/kg to fill the caloric target.

Meal timing across Day 14 to Day 8: three main meals and one to two snacks, protein in every feeding at 0.3 to 0.4 g/kg (roughly 25 to 40 g for most male fighters, 20 to 30 g for most female fighters). The last feeding of the day should sit at least 2 hours before bed; sleep quality is a Week 1 lever the cut plan cannot afford to compromise.

**Hydration.** Water at 3.5 to 4.5 L/day plus fluid from food across Week 1. This is the loading phase for the fight-week water cut — the body needs to be well-hydrated going into Day 3 for the aggressive water manipulation to work cleanly. Sodium at 3 to 5 g/day from salted food and one to two [electrolyte drinks with a full sodium-potassium-magnesium profile](https://scythene.com/products/electrolytes-berry?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=short-notice-fight-14-day-camp-protocol) per day.

**Sleep.** Non-negotiable 8.5 to 9 hours in bed per night through Week 1. If the fighter has a family, a job, or an inconsistent sleep pattern, the camp coordinator's job is to lock this in — the rest of the plan collapses without it.

## The 14-day supplement stack

The short-notice window is short enough that most supplement protocols do not have time to load. Three exceptions are legitimate and worth running:

**Creatine loading.** Creatine saturates muscle stores at approximately 5 g/day across 4 weeks or with a loading protocol of 20 g/day (split across 4 doses of 5 g) across 5 to 7 days followed by 5 g/day maintenance. If the fighter is not already creatine-loaded, a 14-day window is enough time to complete a full 5-to-7-day loading protocol in Week 1 and hold maintenance into fight week. Effects on power output, high-intensity repeat efforts, and (relevant here) intracellular water retention are documented. The intracellular water retention is worth 1 to 2 lb of body mass — plan for it in the weight math, but do not skip the load; the power and repeated-effort benefit is real. Take [creatine monohydrate](https://scythene.com/products/creatine-monohydrate?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=short-notice-fight-14-day-camp-protocol) with a carbohydrate feeding to improve uptake. If the fighter is already creatine-loaded from prior camps, hold maintenance and skip the load — the extra water is not free.

**Electrolyte protocol.** Sodium, potassium, and magnesium replacement across the whole camp is not optional in the short-notice window. Sweat loss across Week 1 hard training is meaningful, and the water cut in Week 2 pulls all three minerals in near-plasma concentrations. Running [a full-profile electrolyte formula](https://scythene.com/products/electrolytes-berry?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=short-notice-fight-14-day-camp-protocol) once or twice a day through Week 1 keeps mineral status topped for the fight-week manipulation. Magnesium at 200 to 400 mg/day supports sleep and neuromuscular function through the deficit.

**Protein supplementation to hit the daily total.** Hitting 2.0 to 2.4 g/kg on a caloric deficit through whole-food feedings alone is possible but logistically hard on a compressed timeline. One to two servings of [whey protein](https://scythene.com/products/whey-protein-vanilla?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=short-notice-fight-14-day-camp-protocol) per day fills the gap, hits the leucine threshold at 25 to 30 g per feeding, and does not add residue that has to be cleared in fight week.

Everything else — beta-alanine (needs 4+ weeks to load), sodium bicarbonate (single-dose fight-day protocol only), citrulline malate as a pre-workout (single-session use, not a loading protocol) — is either a fight-day-only tool or does not have time to work in a 14-day window. See [supplements combat athletes actually need](/blog/supplements-combat-athletes-actually-need) for the broader framework and [supplement red flags](/blog/supplement-red-flags-what-dietitians-should-watch-for) for what to avoid under short-notice pressure when a manager or teammate is pushing an "edge."

Under no circumstances add a supplement in a short-notice camp that the fighter has not used before. The 14-day window is not the time to find out a fighter tolerates a new stimulant poorly or has an idiosyncratic response to a herbal extract. New product plus fight-week nervous-system load is how avoidable disasters happen.

## Day 8 to Day 4: fiber and residue taper into water-load ramp

This is the transition phase. The camp shifts from "preserve fitness" to "prepare the body for the aggressive final phase." Three overlapping protocols run:

**Fiber and residue taper.** Beginning on Day 8, drop total fiber intake from the normal 25 to 40 g/day toward 15 g/day by Day 5 and toward 5 to 10 g/day by Day 3. Cut cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, Brussels sprouts), legumes, high-fiber grains, and skin-on fruits. Shift protein toward the higher-digestibility sources (whey isolate, egg, fish, tender chicken and beef; away from tough cuts and processed meats). The goal is to walk into Day 3 with 400 to 800 g of gut content available to clear across the final 48 hours. See [fight week fiber management](/blog/fight-week-fiber-management-cutting-residue-without-cutting-fuel) for the residue math and the specific food-swap protocol.

**Water load.** Beginning on Day 6, increase fluid intake to 5 to 6 L/day through Day 4. This primes the kidneys and the ADH axis to shed water aggressively when the drop happens on Day 3. Do not skip this step — the water cut on Day 3 to Day 1 works dramatically better in a body that has been water-loaded than in one that is walking around chronically undertopped.

**Training taper.** No hard sessions from Day 7 forward. Technique and mobility only, one light conditioning bout on Day 6 or 7 (30 minutes at conversational pace), and no lifting past 50% intensity. The fighter should feel bored and fresh going into Day 3.

**Nutrition-side weight target.** The scale on Day 4 morning should read at (target weight) + (Δ × 60 to 70%). The remaining 30 to 40% of the total drop is the water phase.

## Day 3 to Day 1: the aggressive water cut

The water-cut protocol on 14 days notice is not different from a normal fight-camp water cut, but the physiologic reserve going in is thinner. Every step should be run with an experienced corner and a fighter who has done this before. First-time water cuts on 14 days notice with an inexperienced coach is how fighters end up in the ER.

**Day 3 morning:** Drop fluid intake to 3 L for the day. Continue the residue taper.
**Day 2 morning:** Drop fluid to 1.5 L. Meal size drops; carbohydrate drops to 1 to 1.5 g/kg (glycogen-water release ~3 g water per g glycogen). Sodium starts pulling — drop to 1 g/day.
**Day 1 morning:** Drop fluid to 500 mL. Small carbohydrate-and-protein meals only; no fiber. Sodium at trace levels. Bath salts or Epsom salt soak in the evening pulls another 0.5 to 1% through osmotic gradient. Sauna suit optional depending on how much weight remains.
**Weigh-in morning:** No fluid. Empty bathroom. Sauna suit session or dry sauna at 15-minute intervals with cooling breaks until the weight lands. Weigh in the moment the scale reads clean.

Aggressive but defensible on a well-conditioned fighter with reserve going in. Not defensible on a fighter walking into Day 3 already depleted, sleep-deprived, or with a Δ that never fit the timeline in the first place.

## Fight day: no experimentation

The fight-day nutrition plan on a short-notice camp is exactly the fighter's normal fight-day plan — nothing new, nothing being tested for the first time. Standard rehydration through the 24 hours between weigh-in and fight, standard pre-fight meal at 4 to 6 hours out (typically a moderate-carb, low-fiber, low-fat meal the fighter has eaten dozens of times), standard walkout fueling in the 60 minutes before the ring (see [60-minute walkout fueling protocol](/blog/60-minute-walkout-fueling-protocol)), standard corner-bag setup (see [cornerman nutrition bag fight night](/blog/cornerman-nutrition-bag-fight-night)).

The two fight-day tools that are legitimately worth deploying on a well-conditioned fighter — caffeine at 3 to 6 mg/kg 45 to 60 minutes before the walk (see [caffeine half-life fight day](/blog/caffeine-half-life-fight-day) for the timing math) and sodium bicarbonate at 0.3 g/kg split-dose across the 90 to 60 minute pre-fight window (see [sodium bicarbonate buffering](/blog/sodium-bicarbonate-buffering-for-combat-athletes) — only run this if the fighter has done it in training before) — are the same tools the fighter would use on a normal camp. Nothing new on 14 days.

## When to turn the fight down

The hardest coaching call in short-notice fighting is when the answer is no. The five hard-no scenarios:

**Δ above 9% of body mass.** The math does not fit inside the safe envelope on this timeline. Catch-weight negotiation or decline.

**Fighter walking around out of shape.** No amount of nutrition planning replaces the sparring and conditioning weeks that were missed. A fighter whose last hard round was 6 weeks ago is not a fighter you take a fight for on 14 days.

**Active injury, illness, or biomarker deficit.** A ferritin at 18, a lingering shoulder that has not been sparring-tested, a URI from last week — every one is a signal the physiologic reserve is not there for a short-notice cut on top.

**First-time water cut on this timeline.** A fighter who has never done a fight-week water cut before should not learn it on 14 days notice. The margin for error is zero.

**A history of failed short-notice fights.** If the fighter has done this before and lost fights on the back of a bad short-notice cut, the second attempt without changed circumstances is a repeat error.

Turning down a short-notice fight preserves career length, preserves the fighter's ability to take the next real camp seriously, and preserves the trust between fighter and coach. The purse from a fight that ends with a hollow-eyed weigh-in and a first-round loss is not worth what the fight costs in the year after.

## The bottom line

A short-notice fight is not a compressed 8-week camp. It is a different problem with a different playbook. The 14-day timeline forces a hard-eyed inventory before the contract signs, a weight-differential math that decides whether the fight is takeable at all, a Week 1 that preserves fitness and drops nutrition-side weight without adding new fatigue, a transition phase that primes the body for the aggressive Day 3-to-1 water cut, a fight-week protocol that runs the standard rehydration and pre-fight tools without introducing anything new, and a fight-day plan that reads exactly like the fighter's normal fight-day plan on any other camp.

The fighter who walks into the Monday phone call with a supplemented, well-conditioned, biomarker-solid, sparring-recent, in-fight-camp-shape body and a differential under 6% is takeable. The fighter with a differential above 9%, a stale training block behind him, a borderline ferritin, or a compressed timeline that never fit is not. The coaching call is deciding which fighter you are looking at, and the responsible answer for the second fighter is a catch-weight negotiation or a decline — not a Hail Mary cut that costs him the fight and costs him the next six months of recovery.

The short-notice camp that gets run right builds career capital. The one that gets run wrong ends careers early. The 14-day protocol above is the version that gets it right on the fighters who are actually in position to take the call.
