Everyone writes about weight cuts. Walk into any combat-sports library — magazines, podcasts, blog archives, YouTube — and the content weight sits firmly in the lower weight classes. The 155-pound lightweight dropping from 175 to 155 in a 10-week camp is the archetype the whole nutrition literature has grown around: the week-by-week body-fat drop, the fight-week water manipulation, the carb-and-sodium reload, the rehydration math. The lower-weight playbook has become so dominant that most fighters, most coaches, and most dietitians assume it is the only playbook.

For the heavyweight fighter — the 240-pound boxing heavyweight who doesn't have an upper limit, the open-weight amateur BJJ competitor who hits every tournament at walking-around weight, the no-cut MMA heavyweight whose "cut" is a 72-hour sodium-and-water trim from 268 to 265, the UFC Heavyweight or ONE Heavyweight whose division ceiling sits above most athletes' hypertrophy capacity — the lower-weight playbook is the wrong playbook. Applying it causes real damage. Depleting glycogen the way a 155er depletes to make weight leaves a heavyweight gassed in round two. Cutting calories to make the lower-end of the division is the wrong intervention for a fighter whose problem is not weight but body-fat distribution. Rehydrating with a protocol built for a 15-pound water cut is irrelevant to a fighter who shed three pounds of sweat in a sauna cameo for show.

Heavyweight nutrition is a different problem with a different protocol. Here is the full playbook: how to think about the walking-around weight when it IS the fight weight, how to track body composition without obsessing over the scale number, how caloric intake scales with lean mass, what the supplement stack actually earns for a 240-pound athlete, how glycogen loading works when storage capacity is a function of muscle mass rather than a function of a depleted state, how fight-week programming looks when there is no cut, and what the real heavyweight nutritional risk is — the one that costs careers.

## The scale is not the problem

The first move in heavyweight nutrition programming is to stop treating the scale as the primary feedback instrument. A 240-pound fighter whose body fat drifts from 14% to 19% over a 20-week off-season looks almost the same on the scale (240 vs 243) and looks almost the same in street clothes. On fight night, in round three, the two fighters are completely different athletes. The 14%-BF fighter is carrying ~206 pounds of lean mass and 34 pounds of fat. The 19%-BF fighter is carrying ~197 pounds of lean mass and 46 pounds of fat. The 12 extra pounds of adipose do not help the fighter take or deliver a punch. The 9 pounds of lost lean mass means less contractile tissue for the strength work and less glycogen-storage capacity for the conditioning work.

The lightweight dietitian reads the scale drop as the primary signal. The heavyweight dietitian reads the body-composition shift as the primary signal and treats the scale as a confounded, lagging indicator that tells you something, but not the thing that matters.

**The heavyweight body-composition targets** vary by sport, division ceiling, and style, but broadly:
- Boxing heavyweight (no upper limit, above 200 lb): 10-14% BF for aggressive pressure fighters, 12-16% for defensive mobility fighters, up to 18% for pure power punchers who trade size for one-shot leverage.
- MMA heavyweight (205-265 lb window): 12-16% BF is the standard target range. Below 12% is uncommon in the actual division because the metabolic cost of maintenance is high.
- MMA openweight BJJ (adults, super-heavy and above): 15-20% BF, with the trade-off that extra mass helps top pressure and sweep defense but costs cardio in longer matches.
- Strongman and powerlifting heavyweight who cross-trains in combat sports: 18-25% BF, trading composition for raw force output.

**The measurement cadence**: body composition every 2-4 weeks across camp using a method you can repeat consistently. DEXA every 8-12 weeks if the budget allows. The in-gym method — skinfolds, bioelectrical impedance, or a tape measure plus body-fat navy formula — is less accurate but tracks the TREND, which is what matters. Pick one method and stick with it; do not compare across methods.

## Caloric intake scales with lean mass, not with scale weight

The default caloric estimate most dietitians use is the Harris-Benedict or Mifflin-St Jeor equation applied to body weight plus an activity multiplier. For a 240-lb fighter in a hard training block, those equations produce estimates in the 3,800-4,500 kcal range depending on exact body comp and training volume. That estimate is roughly correct for a lean 240-pounder — a 206-lb lean-mass fighter whose metabolic machinery is doing the burn work.

But if the fighter is 240 lb at 20% body fat, carrying 192 lb of lean mass, the real resting metabolic rate is closer to a lean 220-pounder's. The Harris-Benedict estimate overshoots by 200-350 kcal/day. The fighter who eats to the overshoot estimate drifts into caloric surplus, which shows up as more body fat, which widens the gap between the equation and reality, which deepens the surplus. The spiral is slow — 1-2 pounds of fat per month — but across a 20-week camp it adds 10-15 pounds of nonfunctional mass that the fighter is carrying into the ring.

**The heavyweight caloric framework**:
- Estimate from lean body mass, not scale weight. Use a formula like `(13.75 × lean mass in lb) + 400` for RMR and a 1.5-1.9 activity multiplier for the hard-training heavyweight depending on exact session volume.
- Verify empirically against the scale and body-comp trend across 2-3 weeks before committing to the number. If the scale drifts up and body comp holds, you are over by the drift amount × 3,500 kcal / # days.
- Protein intake 1.6-2.0 g/kg of total body weight for a heavyweight — not 2.4+ g/kg of the ultra-high-protein hypertrophy stacks that get recommended for leaner weight-cutting fighters. The 240-lb fighter does not need 260g of protein daily. 175-220g is sufficient for recovery, and the extra space in the budget belongs to carbohydrate for glycogen and to dietary fat for hormonal baseline.
- Carbohydrate 4-7 g/kg across the training week, periodized to session intensity. Heavyweight glycogen storage capacity is substantial — a 206-lb-lean-mass fighter can store 600+ g of muscle glycogen when fully loaded, which is 2,400+ kcal of fuel sitting in the muscles before any food intake on fight day.

## The supplement stack earns differently at heavyweight

The supplement stack for a lightweight fighter mostly earns on cardio-performance and recovery margins. The same stack applied to a heavyweight earns a different ROI because the mass economics are different.

**[Creatine monohydrate](https://scythene.com/supplements/creatine-monohydrate?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=heavyweight-nutrition-programming-without-a-weight-cut)** is the clearest heavyweight-ROI supplement. The muscle creatine pool scales with lean mass, so a heavyweight loading creatine stores more total phosphocreatine than a lighter fighter does, which translates to more work capacity in the short-high-intensity bursts that decide heavyweight rounds — clinching, pressure phases, scramble work on the ground. The standard 3-5 g/day maintenance dose works for most heavyweights; some sports-nutrition work supports going slightly higher at 5-7 g/day for athletes above 200 lb lean mass. Load with 20 g/day × 5-7 days if you want faster saturation, or just run 5 g/day and reach saturation at ~4 weeks. [Creatine is the most-researched supplement in sports](/blog/creatine-the-most-researched-supplement-in-sports) for a reason.

**[Whey protein](https://scythene.com/supplements/whey-protein?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=heavyweight-nutrition-programming-without-a-weight-cut)** earns its stack slot not because heavyweight protein targets are higher per kg (they are not — they are often lower per kg) but because hitting 180-220 g of protein from whole food alone is a significant GI load. A heavyweight who eats 4 whole-food meals a day plus a whey shake post-training is distributing the protein load more digestibly than a heavyweight trying to hit the same total from food alone, which keeps the GI from getting in the way of the training.

**[Omega-3 fish oil](https://scythene.com/supplements/omega-3?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=heavyweight-nutrition-programming-without-a-weight-cut)** earns its stack slot in heavyweights because the inflammatory load from heavy sparring at 240 lb is nontrivial — joint stress, impact load, and the recovery demand from larger muscles taking longer to turn over all push the resolution-of-inflammation pathways. 2-3 g/day of combined EPA+DHA from a disclosed-dose product is the standard protocol. The lightweight whose joints have taken less lifetime impact may skip it. The 35-year-old heavyweight who has been training since high school has usually accumulated the micro-injury history that the omega-3 intervention addresses.

**What heavyweights usually do not need from the stack**:
- Pre-workout stimulant blends. Heavyweight cardio capacity is often the limiting factor, and the heart-rate-elevating effect of 300+ mg of caffeine plus the vasoconstrictive effects of some pre-workout ingredients can tighten the breathing in a sparring session where the fighter's already working above threshold. A plain 150-200 mg caffeine dose from coffee or a dialed-in product is cleaner. (See [caffeine for members](/blog/caffeine-for-members-a-complete-guide) for dose and timing.)
- Fat burners / thermogenics. The heavyweight whose problem is body-fat drift does not fix the drift with a fat burner. The fix is caloric budgeting and training-load management. The stimulant stack adds HR load without fixing the underlying problem.

## Glycogen loading at heavyweight

The classic carb-loading protocol was developed on marathon runners and later adapted to combat sports. Most of the published protocols — supercompensation across 3-6 days, moderate deplete-then-load, or straight high-carb loading — assume a lighter athlete with ~400 g of total muscle glycogen storage capacity.

A heavyweight has roughly 1.5× that capacity by virtue of carrying more muscle. The glycogen math at 206 lb of lean mass:

- Full storage: ~600-650 g muscle glycogen + ~100 g liver glycogen = ~700-750 g total
- At 4 kcal/g: ~2,800-3,000 kcal of stored fuel
- Depleted state: ~300-400 g total
- The load capacity: ~400 g of carbohydrate converted to glycogen across the 72-hour reload window, assuming adequate insulin, hydration, and intake pacing

For a heavyweight who IS NOT cutting, the straight-high-carb-loading protocol is cleaner than the deplete-then-load protocol. There is no reason to deplete first — the fighter was not depleted at the start of fight week. Simply ramp daily carb intake from the training-week baseline (4-5 g/kg) to the fight-day baseline (7-10 g/kg) across the 72 hours before weigh-in or competition.

**The practical protocol**:
- 72 hours out: 6-7 g carb/kg body weight. For a 240-lb fighter, ~650-750 g carb (which is a lot — pasta, rice, oatmeal, potatoes, bagels, fruit, sports drink in training).
- 48 hours out: 7-8 g carb/kg. ~750-850 g carb.
- 24 hours out: 8-10 g carb/kg. ~850-1,050 g carb. This is where the fighter should feel visibly "full" — the GI load is substantial, so lower-fiber choices (white rice, white pasta, bagels, sports drinks, sweet potatoes without skin, bananas) replace the higher-fiber training-week choices.
- Fight morning: a 500-700 kcal breakfast focused on familiar carbs (oatmeal, toast, banana, honey) plus a lean protein (eggs, turkey, Greek yogurt). Target eating 3-4 hours before the walkout.

The sodium pairing matters at heavyweight because the heavier athlete also carries more sweat volume capacity. Fight-week sodium should be 4-6 g/day across the loading window — supporting plasma volume, enabling the glycogen to pull water into the muscle, and getting ahead of the salt loss in the warmup. Potassium 3-4 g/day as the paired electrolyte.

## Fight-week programming without a cut

The lightweight fight-week playbook is 60% a weight-cut-and-rehydration protocol and 40% a glycogen-and-cardio protocol. For the heavyweight who is not cutting, delete the entire 60% and double-emphasize the 40%.

**Day 7 to Day 4 (fight week open)**:
- Training volume drops to roughly 60% of camp max. One hard sparring day (Monday or Tuesday for Saturday fight), one hard strength day, and three low-intensity movement/skill sessions.
- Nutrition baseline. Hitting daily caloric target, keeping protein steady, maintaining normal hydration. Nothing fancy.
- Sleep gets protected. 8-9 hours in bed with blackout and cool room. The sleep is where recovery banks for fight night.

**Day 3 to Day 1 (loading window)**:
- Training drops to light skill work and warmup-tempo sessions only. No sparring, no heavy strength.
- Carb intake ramps as above. The GI should feel heavy by Day 1 — this is the signal the fuel is loading.
- Fiber drops from the training-week baseline (typically 35-45 g/day) to 20-25 g/day. The lower-fiber choices reduce GI residue and keep the fighter from carrying an uncomfortable gut into the ring.
- Hydration: 3-4 L water per day plus the sports drink intake, with sodium at 4-6 g/day and potassium at 3-4 g/day.

**Day 0 (fight day)**:
- Breakfast 3-4 hours before walkout: 500-700 kcal, familiar carbs plus lean protein, 500 mL fluid.
- 90 minutes before walkout: 20-30 g simple carbs (banana + honey, sports drink, or a sport-nutrition gel), 300-400 mL fluid, 150-200 mg caffeine if that is the fighter's dialed-in dose.
- Walkout: ready, loaded, hydrated. No tricks required.

The heavyweight who follows this protocol walks to the ring at full glycogen, full hydration, and the body-comp number they trained to across camp. There is no reason for anything else to happen in the final 72 hours.

## The real heavyweight risk

The lightweight fighter's primary nutritional risk is a bad weight cut — showing up gassed and dehydrated to the point of concussion-risk-elevation and compromised cardio. The heavyweight has no equivalent risk from a cut because there is no cut. The real heavyweight nutritional risk is slower and quieter: creeping body-fat gain across years that costs the fighter one round of cardio per percent of body-fat drift.

The pattern is:
- Fighter starts career at 230 lb, 12% BF.
- Across 3 years and 8 fights, body comp drifts to 240 lb at 16%.
- Cardio in round three gets noticeably worse. Coaches read it as "aging" or "ring rust."
- The fighter responds with more cardio training, which does not address the underlying caloric budget issue.
- Across another 2 years, body comp drifts to 248 lb at 19%. Fighter is now 4 years into a career with 18 extra pounds of fat and 7 fewer pounds of lean mass than he started with.
- The fights where he dominates ring-center pressure for the first 5 minutes and then dies in round two are the pattern. The gym explains it as "he just needs to work harder." The nutrition explanation is 15 pounds of nonfunctional mass he is carrying through every round.

**The fix is prevention, not rescue**:
- Measure body composition quarterly year-round, not just in camp.
- Hold the drift target at ±1-2% BF across the year. A 14%-BF fighter who hits 16% at any quarterly check gets an off-season body-recomp block in the next quarter, not a weight-cut rescue in the fight camp.
- Audit the off-season diet. Most heavyweight body-fat drift happens in the 4-8 weeks between fights when training volume drops but caloric intake does not. The off-season deficit of 300-500 kcal/day is the correct intervention, held for the full between-fight window.
- Protein stays the same between fights. Carbohydrate drops with training volume. Fat holds. The fighter does not lose the body-fat drift by cutting protein — that is how lean mass gets lost.

## What this looks like at camp

A representative week for a 240-lb MMA heavyweight at 14% BF, 6 weeks out from the fight, might run:

**Daily totals**: 4,200 kcal, 185 g protein, 530 g carb, 125 g fat.

**Monday — Hard sparring day**:
- Breakfast: Oatmeal with Greek yogurt, berries, honey, almonds. ~800 kcal.
- Mid-morning: Protein shake (whey + banana + oats). ~400 kcal.
- Pre-training: Rice + chicken + vegetables + olive oil. ~700 kcal.
- Training 1 hour: Sparring + pad work. ~1,000 kcal burn.
- Post-training: Protein shake + sports drink + banana. ~500 kcal.
- Dinner: Salmon + sweet potato + salad + olive oil. ~900 kcal.
- Evening: Cottage cheese + fruit. ~300 kcal.
- Sodium ~4 g, potassium ~4 g, fluid ~4 L.

**Wednesday — Easy skill day**:
- Same meal structure, carb intake drops ~15% because training load is lower. Protein and fat hold constant.

**Saturday — Rest day**:
- Lower carb (~400 g), protein and fat held. Caloric budget drops ~500 kcal because of the lower training-day burn. This is where the body-comp management happens — the rest days without the training burn are where the surplus-to-fat conversion happens if the budget is not controlled.

## Wrap-up

Heavyweight nutrition is a different sport from lightweight nutrition. The dominant content library is wrong for the heavyweight audience, and applying it causes real damage: unnecessary depletion, excess cardio prescription for what is actually a body-comp problem, misread body-composition tracking, and the slow drift into carrying 15 pounds of nonfunctional mass that costs rounds and shortens careers.

The playbook is:
1. The walking-around weight is the fight weight. The scale is a lagging indicator. Body composition is the primary feedback signal.
2. Caloric intake scales with lean body mass, not with scale weight. Protein, carbohydrate, and fat all have different ratios than the lightweight world assumes.
3. The supplement stack earns differently — creatine ROI is highest, pre-workout stimulants are usually net-negative, and omega-3 earns its slot for the recovery and inflammation load that heavier training produces.
4. Fight week is a straight glycogen-and-hydration load, no cut, no manipulation. Carb intake ramps to 8-10 g/kg across the 72 hours before walkout.
5. The real risk is slow body-fat drift between fights. Prevention is quarterly body-comp monitoring year-round, not camp-only.

Train at the weight you compete at. Load for fight night. Audit the body-comp trend across years. The heavyweight who runs the correct playbook keeps their round-three cardio intact for the full length of their career.
