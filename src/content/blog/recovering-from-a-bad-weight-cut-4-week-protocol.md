A 145-lb featherweight makes weight for his last fight on the back of a brutal cut: 12 lbs pulled in the final 72 hours, an overnight sauna suit session, a diuretic he does not admit to using, and a Friday morning weigh-in where he steps on the scale at 144.8 and immediately vomits the sip of water he was allowed. He passes the physical, gets the belt to the doctor's arm's length from the scale, and walks out looking like someone who just donated three liters of blood. He rehydrates through the 24 hours before his fight, gets to Saturday feeling roughly 80% recovered, comes out sharp in round one, and gasses at the two-minute mark of round two. He wins on the cards but he knows he shouldn't have. Fight ends. He falls asleep in the car. He wakes up Monday feeling like he got run over.

He has 7 weeks until his next fight. His manager is already talking about the walk-up. His coach wants him in the gym Tuesday. The temptation is to shake off the cut damage across the weekend, get back into camp Monday, and start pulling weight down again at Week 4.

That path guarantees the next cut is worse than the last one.

The cut he just ran did not just move water and food out. It depleted glycogen stores by 60-80%, evacuated 400-800 g of gut content, pulled 2-4 kg of intracellular and extracellular water, dumped electrolytes he cannot fully replete in 24 hours, disrupted sleep architecture for 3-5 days on either side, elevated inflammatory markers, and stressed the HPA axis on top of the training-load damage the camp itself put down. The rehydration window between weigh-in and fight refilled some of it, but not all. The body he brought into fight night is not the body he trained through camp with — it is a partially rehydrated, glycogen-under-repleted, sleep-debt-carrying version.

Between fights, the recovery protocol matters. The fighter who runs a 4-week structured recovery before starting the next camp shows up to Week 4 of the new cut with body weight higher (in the correct direction), glycogen restored, mineral balance restored, and training capacity restored to what it was at the start of the last camp. The fighter who skips the recovery and starts cutting again from a compromised baseline runs a harder cut on less physiologic reserve, and if he does it repeatedly, walks himself into the chronic under-fueled, over-trained pattern that ends fighter careers early.

Here is the 4-week protocol.

## The damage inventory (week 1)

The first thing to do is name what has to be recovered. Every hard weight cut leaves the fighter behind in the same six compartments; the recovery plan works one compartment at a time.

**Hydration status.** Post-cut plasma volume is typically down 8-15% for 3-7 days after the fight even with aggressive rehydration. Total-body water can lag longer if the cut pulled more than 5% of body mass in the final 48 hours. Signs the hydration status is still off: dark or amber urine on morning voids, low urine volume, orthostatic dizziness on standing, dry mouth, elevated resting heart rate.

**Glycogen.** Muscle glycogen replenishes at 5-7 g/kg body mass per day with aggressive carbohydrate intake (7-10 g/kg/day) and rest, meaning a 65-kg fighter with post-fight glycogen at 60% depletion needs 3-5 days of high-carbohydrate intake and low training load to fully refill. Liver glycogen replenishes faster (24-48 hours) but is limited to about 100 g of storage total.

**Mineral balance — sodium, potassium, magnesium.** Sauna cuts and diuretic-assisted cuts pull sodium and potassium in near-plasma concentrations, and magnesium is depleted through sweat loss and increased urinary excretion. Post-fight symptoms of mineral under-repletion: muscle cramps, twitches, poor sleep quality, low mood, and fatigue that does not resolve with rest.

**Sleep and HPA axis.** Cortisol is elevated for 3-7 days post-fight from the combined weight-cut stress, competition stress, and (if applicable) head-impact stress. Sleep architecture is disrupted; slow-wave sleep and REM percentages both drop for 4-10 days. The fighter's subjective wellness usually reads as flat or depressed for 5-14 days.

**Musculoskeletal damage.** The fight itself put down eccentric loading, striking impacts, grappling stress, and (in most cases) at least mild concussive impact. Delayed-onset soreness peaks at 48-72 hours; residual inflammation and micro-damage clears across 7-14 days.

**Immune function.** The exercise-immunology literature is consistent that hard competition drops immune function for 3-14 days post-event. Upper respiratory infections are common in the two weeks after fights, and the weight cut compounds the drop.

The Week 1 job is to run repletion on all six compartments before doing anything else.

## Week 1: repletion, not training

**Days 1-2 (Sunday, Monday after Friday weigh-in / Saturday fight):** No training. Sleep 9-10 hours if the body will take it. Eat to appetite, high carbohydrate (6-8 g/kg body mass), moderate protein (1.6-2.0 g/kg), fat as tolerated. Fluid to satisfy thirst plus 500-1000 mL/day extra with sodium. Nothing complicated. If the fighter is craving pizza and ice cream, this is the two days where those cravings are the biological signal of glycogen and salt depletion talking; feed the signal.

**Days 3-4 (Tuesday, Wednesday):** Light movement only — a 20-30 minute walk, light stretching, a light mobility circuit. No sparring, no lifting, no conditioning. Carbohydrate stays high (6-8 g/kg), protein at 1.6-2.0 g/kg, fluid at maintenance plus 500 mL/day above baseline for the first week.

**Days 5-7 (Thursday, Friday, Saturday):** Light training returns — a technique-only session, a light lifting session (50% intensity), a low-intensity conditioning bout of 30-40 minutes. No hard rounds, no metcons, no heavy loads. This is a movement-and-blood-flow week, not a training week.

**Mineral repletion.** Sodium at 3-5 g/day from salt on food and electrolyte drinks — do not restrict salt after a hard cut. A daily electrolyte drink through the first 5-7 days replaces sodium, potassium, and magnesium the cut pulled; a scoop of [electrolytes with a full sodium-potassium-magnesium profile](https://scythene.com/products/electrolytes-berry?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=recovering-from-a-bad-weight-cut-4-week-protocol) in water once or twice a day through the first week is the routine that works. Magnesium supplementation at 200-400 mg/day of a bioavailable form supports sleep, muscle recovery, and mineral repletion — [magnesium bisglycinate](https://scythene.com/products/magnesium-bisglycinate?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=recovering-from-a-bad-weight-cut-4-week-protocol) at bedtime is the form most fighters tolerate best, and it does not carry the osmotic-laxative effect the citrate has.

**Sleep discipline.** Prioritize 9-10 hours in bed for Week 1. Dark room, cool temperature, no phone in the last hour before sleep. Sleep is the largest lever on cortisol recovery, glycogen restoration, and mood.

**No stepping on the scale.** Weight will rebound in the first 3-5 days as glycogen, water, and gut content restore. Trying to control the rebound with under-eating is the single largest mistake fighters make in this window. The 8-12 lb rebound in the first week is expected, normal, and required for the fighter to be at a training baseline for camp. Any body-recomposition work belongs to Weeks 3-4, not Week 1.

## Week 2: training returns, appetite normalizes

**Training load returns to 50-70% of pre-camp normal.** Full training week — technique, sparring, lifting, conditioning — but volume and intensity capped at roughly 60% of the pre-camp normal week. No hard sparring rounds, no max lifts, no all-out conditioning. This is a re-entry week.

**Nutrition normalizes.** Carbohydrate drops from the deliberate over-eating of Week 1 to maintenance for the fighter's training load — usually 5-7 g/kg for a moderate training week. Protein stays at 1.6-2.2 g/kg. Fat as tolerated. Total intake is at or slightly above maintenance; the fighter should not be in a deficit at any point in Weeks 1-3.

**Body weight settles at the "walk-around" or off-camp baseline.** For a fighter who competes at 145, this is typically 158-170 depending on how much cut he ran. The walk-around weight in the 12-25% margin above the competition weight is the healthy range; more than 25% is a signal the off-camp weight has crept and the fighter is stacking harder cuts each time.

**Sleep, HRV, and subjective wellness normalize.** By the end of Week 2, morning HRV should be back to the pre-camp baseline or better, subjective wellness should read as normal-plus-recovered, and appetite should be normal-to-slightly-elevated. If HRV is still suppressed, if the fighter is still craving salt and sugar in unusual amounts, if sleep quality has not restored — the recovery is behind schedule and Week 3 should not push training load up.

**Bloodwork if indicated.** For fighters with a history of iron deficiency, low T, or other under-recovery markers, a Week 2 draw for a basic panel (CBC, CMP, iron studies for female fighters and any male fighter with a prior low finding, testosterone panel if the fighter is on the older side or has ever had a low read) can catch the compensating pattern before Week 4. See [mid-camp bloodwork for fighters](/blog/mid-camp-bloodwork-for-fighters) for the panel discussion.

## Week 3: full training, body-composition work if warranted

**Training load returns to 100% of pre-camp normal.** Full sparring, full lifting, full conditioning. No cut, no deficit. If the fighter's next camp starts at Week 4, this is the last week of unrestricted eating.

**Body composition assessment.** This is the decision point on the next camp's cut depth. Weigh the fighter Monday and Thursday morning of Week 3 in the same conditions. If the fighter is walking around at 15-18% above competition weight, the next cut is a moderate one and the camp starts with a small deficit. If the fighter is at 20-25%, the camp starts sooner with a larger daily deficit. If the fighter is above 25%, the camp needs to start immediately at Week 4 with a substantial deficit — and honestly, this is the fighter for whom the coach and manager conversation about moving up a weight class deserves to happen now, not after the next brutal cut.

**Protein intake.** If body-composition work is warranted, protein bumps to 2.0-2.4 g/kg to preserve lean mass under any caloric deficit. A dedicated post-training [whey protein isolate](https://scythene.com/products/whey-protein-vanilla?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=recovering-from-a-bad-weight-cut-4-week-protocol) shake at 30-40 g provides the recovery-window protein pulse without adding meaningful fat or carbohydrate to the daily total.

**Micronutrient audit.** By Week 3, the fighter who has been eating whole foods heavy in green vegetables, lean protein, and moderate whole grains is repleting the micronutrient deficits the cut and camp put down. Fighters on restrictive eating patterns, plant-based diets, or low-variety patterns should audit iron, zinc, magnesium, vitamin D, and B12 status through a bloodwork panel if not done in Week 2.

## Week 4: bridge to the next camp

**Training load: pre-camp full normal.** The fighter is training at the intensity and volume the next camp will begin at.

**Nutrition: bridge to the camp deficit.** For fighters starting a cut, the transition from maintenance to camp-deficit intake happens gradually over 4-7 days rather than as a Monday cliff. This preserves training capacity and prevents the acute glycogen crash that undermines the first week of the new camp.

**Sleep, HRV, wellness: all normal.** The go/no-go criteria for starting the next camp:

- HRV back to pre-camp baseline for 7 consecutive days.
- Sleep quality normal, subjective wellness normal-to-good.
- Training capacity — lifts back to previous max, conditioning back to previous baseline.
- Body weight stable at the walk-around baseline (not drifting up or down without explanation).
- Appetite normal, no unusual cravings.
- No lingering musculoskeletal injury from the last camp.
- No lingering symptom cluster from the last cut (fatigue, mood suppression, low libido, cold intolerance).

If all seven are green, start the next camp. If any one is not green, delay the camp by a week and continue the recovery-and-monitoring pattern; if two or more are not green, this is a longer conversation with the coach and (if available) a sports dietitian about whether the fighter is in the compensating under-recovery pattern that eventually ends careers. See [why fighters lose strength during weight cuts](/blog/why-fighters-lose-strength-during-weight-cuts) for the pattern.

## The specific mistakes to avoid

**Weighing in daily during Week 1.** The rebound is the physiologic goal, not a failure. Any scale check inside 5-7 days of the cut misreads the signal and drives the fighter toward under-eating in exactly the window where over-eating is the physiologic requirement.

**Restarting training too hard in Week 1.** The first-week body is not fully rehydrated, not glycogen-restocked, not immune-recovered, and not HPA-normalized. Hard training in Week 1 extends the recovery timeline by 3-7 days.

**Skipping the sodium and electrolyte repletion.** The post-cut fighter who eats "clean" without the salt and electrolyte input the cut requires will crash in Week 2 with headaches, dizziness, and low training capacity that reads as under-recovery when the actual driver is unreplenished minerals.

**Under-sleeping.** The 9-10-hour Week 1 sleep target is not luxury — it is the primary lever on cortisol normalization and glycogen restoration.

**Starting the next camp at Week 2 or Week 3.** The 4-week window is not conservative; it is the minimum for the fighter who ran a hard cut. Fighters who consistently start next-camp cuts at Week 2 accumulate the compensating pattern within 2-4 fights and start losing at Week 8 of camps that used to work at Week 6.

**Ignoring the HRV and wellness signal.** If subjective wellness and HRV are not restored by Week 3, the fighter is not ready to cut again. Pushing anyway produces the compounding-damage pattern.

**Stacking a second bad cut on top of a first.** The single largest predictor of a catastrophic next cut is an under-recovered previous cut. Every fighter, coach, and manager reading this knows an athlete who ran two bad cuts in a row and had to be pulled from the third fight because the body could not go. The 4-week protocol is the insurance against that pattern.

## When to move up a weight class

Not every fighter can walk down to their current class. The signal that the class is wrong:

- Walk-around weight consistently above 25% of competition weight for two consecutive camps.
- Every cut in the last three fights ran deeper than planned or required rescue interventions (extra sauna, extra diuretic, IV rehydration).
- Post-cut symptoms take longer than 2 weeks to fully resolve.
- Bloodwork consistently shows suppressed hormones (low T, suppressed thyroid, low libido) between camps.
- Performance in the fight itself deteriorates in the later rounds despite adequate training.
- Coach and cornermen consistently report the fighter looks depleted at weigh-in.

Moving up a weight class is not a career setback. It is the fighter recognizing that the compensating pattern has costs that exceed the tactical advantage of the smaller class. Have the conversation with your coach honestly and early, ideally at Week 3 of the recovery — not after the next brutal cut.

## The bottom line

A bad weight cut is not just a fight-night event; it is a metabolic and physiologic hole the fighter walks out of. The 4-week protocol between fights is the structured refill of the hole — hydration, glycogen, minerals, sleep, HPA axis, musculoskeletal recovery, immune function — before the next camp starts pulling from the same well.

Week 1: repletion, not training. Week 2: training returns to 50-70%, nutrition normalizes. Week 3: full training, body-composition work if warranted, bloodwork if warranted. Week 4: bridge to the next camp with the go/no-go check against seven criteria.

The fighter who runs the recovery ends up with a smaller cut next time, better performance on fight night, and a career that outlasts the compounding-damage pattern. The one who skips it lines up the next brutal cut, then the one after that, and eventually the day when the body says no.
