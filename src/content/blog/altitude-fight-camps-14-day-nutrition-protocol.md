The pitch for an altitude training block is real but narrow. You go to 2,000-to-2,500 meters for two to three weeks, your kidneys sense the lower oxygen partial pressure, your body releases erythropoietin, your bone marrow pushes red cell production, and when you drop back to sea level for fight week, you have 3 to 5% more hemoglobin mass hauling oxygen to the working muscle. For a fighter pushing a full 15-minute championship pace, that is not nothing. It is also not automatic. The adaptation depends on iron status being adequate for new red cell production, on carbohydrate intake being high enough to fuel the physiologically harder training, on hydration holding against the dry mountain air, and on sleep recovering past the first three to five nights of fragmented architecture.

Half of altitude blocks fail quietly because the nutrition plan is the same one the fighter was running at sea level. Here is the 14-day protocol that makes the trip worth the plane ticket.

## What Altitude Actually Does

At 2,000 meters — think Big Bear, Albuquerque, Flagstaff, parts of Boulder — the atmospheric pressure is about 80% of sea level. The oxygen fraction of air is the same (21%), but with lower pressure driving less oxygen across the lung-to-blood interface, arterial oxygen saturation drops from the 97-99% you run at sea level to roughly 90-94% at rest and into the mid-80s under load. Your body reads this as a stress and responds along three timelines.

**0 to 72 hours.** Increased ventilation rate, mild alkalosis (you blow off CO2 faster), fluid shift, elevated resting heart rate, fragmented sleep. Performance drops across every intensity. This is the acute response. It feels bad because it is bad.

**3 to 7 days.** Kidney starts releasing erythropoietin (EPO). 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG) in red cells rises, which improves oxygen offloading at the working muscle. Sleep normalizes. Resting heart rate starts to settle. Performance starts to recover but is not yet above baseline.

**7 to 21 days.** Hemoglobin mass increases — if, and only if, iron substrate is available and the training stimulus is adequate. Buffering capacity improves. Lactate threshold velocity still reads lower than sea-level values (because of the lower absolute oxygen availability during the stay), but the underlying machinery is getting better.

Two to three weeks is the published window where the adaptation payoff is worth the acute performance cost. Shorter than that and you absorb the stress without the return. Longer than four weeks and you start to accumulate detraining because you cannot hit the sea-level training velocities.

## The Four-Week-Out Pre-Camp Bloodwork

This is the one non-negotiable. An altitude block costs you two to three weeks of your camp. If you go up with low iron, the bone marrow cannot make the new red cells, and the trip returns essentially the acute stress without the chronic adaptation. Draw at four weeks out, not two weeks out — you need runway to correct a flagged value before the trip.

The panel:

- **Ferritin.** Target floor for an altitude block is 50 ng/mL for male fighters, 40 ng/mL for female fighters. Standard-of-care "normal" at most labs bottoms out at 15-30. You need higher than that going into altitude.
- **Hemoglobin and hematocrit.** Baseline so the post-trip redraw is interpretable.
- **sTfR (soluble transferrin receptor).** If ferritin is borderline, sTfR distinguishes iron-restricted erythropoiesis from functional adequacy. A high sTfR with low-to-normal ferritin means the bone marrow is already iron-starved and altitude will make it worse.
- **Vitamin D (25-OH-D).** Below 30 ng/mL affects muscle recovery; altitude is a bigger stress than sea-level training and the margin for recovery shrinks.

If ferritin is below the altitude-trip floor, the four-week window is your repletion runway. Oral iron at 100 mg elemental every other day, taken fasted with vitamin C, is the dosing pattern that outperforms daily dosing for ferritin movement. A redraw at two weeks out confirms the trajectory before you book the flight.

## The Carbohydrate Shift at Altitude

At the same submaximal intensity, your body oxidizes a greater percentage of carbohydrate and a lower percentage of fat at altitude than at sea level. The exact mechanism is debated (sympathetic tone, hypoxia-sensitive enzymes in the mitochondrion, altered substrate preference), but the training implication is clean: your carb demand goes up at the same training load.

Target daily carbohydrate at altitude:

- **Rest or recovery day:** 5-6 g/kg bodyweight
- **Technical / drilling day:** 6-7 g/kg
- **Hard session day (sparring, long run, interval work):** 8-10 g/kg

A 170-pound fighter at 8 g/kg on a hard sparring day is eating 620 g of carbohydrate — roughly 2,480 kcal from carbohydrate alone. If you run the sea-level 4-5 g/kg carb target at altitude, you will show up to the second hard session of the week flat and blame the altitude when the real problem is an intake gap.

## Hydration and the Dry-Air Loss

Mountain air at 2,000 meters is cold and dry. You lose fluid to insensible respiratory losses (every breath comes in dry and goes out saturated with water vapor) at roughly 1.5 to 2 times your sea-level rate. The thirst drive is also blunted at altitude for the first several days, so the lagging indicator you trust at sea level (I am not thirsty, I am fine) stops working.

Target: baseline fluid at 40 mL/kg/day — for a 170-lb fighter, that is 3.1 L — plus 500-750 mL per hour of training, plus an additional 500-1,000 mL/day for the altitude respiratory loss. That lands you at 4-5 L of fluid daily, not counting training sessions. Carry a bottle. Drink to a target, not to thirst, for the first week.

Electrolytes matter more at altitude for two reasons: higher sweat losses from both heat and altitude ventilation, and the acute alkalosis from hyperventilation shifts sodium and potassium handling. Two to three grams of sodium per day above the dietary baseline, scaled with session intensity, is the practical floor.

## The First 72 Hours: Sleep Architecture

The first three to five nights at altitude are fragmented. Periodic breathing — a hypoxia-driven oscillation between hyperventilation and brief apnea — disrupts the normal sleep cycle and cuts deep sleep. You will wake multiple times per night, your sleep-tracking wearable will register a disaster, and you will feel every bit of it the next day.

Nutrition levers that help:

- **Magnesium glycinate 300-400 mg at bedtime.** Supports the GABA-ergic calming pathway. Helps sleep latency. Do not confuse with magnesium citrate, which is a laxative at that dose.
- **High-carb dinner.** Carbohydrate intake two to three hours before bed supports tryptophan transport across the blood-brain barrier and modestly improves sleep latency at altitude.
- **No caffeine past noon.** Caffeine half-life is unchanged, but the margin for sleep disruption is smaller. The late-afternoon espresso that works at sea level does not work here.
- **Alcohol is zero.** Alcohol worsens periodic breathing and amplifies the altitude sleep disruption. There is no "one beer at the cabin" exception for a camp block.

By night four or five, sleep architecture usually normalizes. If it does not, the altitude adaptation itself is probably not landing, and you have a bigger problem than the sleep.

## The Weight Problem

In the first 3 to 7 days at altitude, almost every fighter drops 2 to 4 pounds on the scale. This is a diuretic response to altitude — it is fluid, not fat, and it is the one weight-loss moment at altitude that lies to you the most. The response reverses when you descend. Do not run a weight-cut plan that treats the altitude-induced fluid drop as progress; you will under-fuel a trip that already has a higher carb demand.

Weigh daily in the same conditions, but use the 7-day rolling average for any management decision. The within-trip raw number tells you nothing until you are two full weeks in.

## The 14-Day Framework

- **Day -28 to -14:** pre-camp bloodwork. Repletion if needed. Flight booked only after the ferritin trajectory confirms.
- **Day -7:** final iron, hydration, and carb-loading check. Travel-day meal prep.
- **Day 1-3 (arrival):** training volume at 60-70% of sea-level plan. Carbohydrate at 7-8 g/kg daily regardless of session length. Fluid 4-5 L/day. Magnesium at bedtime. No sparring. No high-intensity intervals.
- **Day 4-7:** training volume to 80-85% of sea-level plan. Add one sparring or hard-interval session. Daily iron (100 mg elemental, every other day, fasted + vitamin C). Reassess sleep, resting heart rate drift, and session RPE.
- **Day 8-14:** training volume at 95-105% of sea-level plan at the lower absolute velocities altitude forces. Two hard sessions per week. Hold hydration, electrolytes, and carb targets. Taper the training load across the final two days before descent.
- **Day 15 (descent) through fight week:** the sea-level adaptation window. Hemoglobin mass peaks approximately 7-14 days post-descent; most of the literature points to 7-10 days as the sweet spot for the fight itself.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Going up without the pre-camp bloodwork.** You are gambling the whole trip on an unknown substrate base.
2. **Running the sea-level carb number.** You will under-fuel and misattribute the fatigue to altitude when it is an intake gap.
3. **Chasing the first-week scale drop.** It is fluid. Do not restrict further because of it.
4. **Treating the first-week sleep disruption as a plan failure.** It is normal. Hold the nutrition protocol. Sleep architecture normalizes by day 4-5.
5. **Timing the return-to-sea-level too close to the fight.** The 7-to-10-day post-descent window is where hemoglobin mass is highest. Flying in the day before weigh-ins wastes the adaptation.
6. **Alcohol.** There is no room for alcohol on an altitude block.

## The Bottom Line

An altitude fight camp is one of the more expensive single protocols in combat sports — time, money, and training stress all go in. The adaptation is real but conditional. Iron status qualifies the trip. Carbohydrate intake fuels the trip. Hydration and electrolytes protect you across the trip. The sleep stack gets you through the first week. The scale lies to you for the first week. And the return-to-sea-level timing decides whether any of it lands on fight night.

Pre-camp bloodwork four weeks out, 14 days at altitude, land back in your gym 7 to 10 days before the fight. That is the protocol. Everything else is details.
