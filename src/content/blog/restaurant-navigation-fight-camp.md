Fight camp is 8 to 12 weeks. Nobody cooks every meal for 12 weeks. The team dinner after Wednesday sparring, the family meal your partner has been asking for, the drive to the sanctioned tournament with three teammates in the truck — restaurant meals are inevitable. What separates the fighter who makes weight from the one who blows up 4 pounds on Wednesday night is not whether they eat out. It is the read they bring to the menu when they do.

I have watched competent fighters lose entire weight-cut plans to one restaurant meal that they thought was "clean." The salad with grilled chicken that hit them with 900 calories of dressing. The sushi that was 55 grams of hidden sugar in the rice and sauces. The "healthy" bowl chain where the base was a rice serving three times the size they estimated. None of these fighters were being reckless. They were making menu reads with the wrong model.

This post is the working framework I use with combat athletes for restaurant navigation during camp: the two decisions that matter before you sit down, the 45-second menu read every fighter should have automatic, the six restaurant categories with their specific traps and the plate builds that actually work, and the recovery protocol when the meal overshoots.

## The Two Decisions Before You Sit Down

Most restaurant damage happens before the menu opens, because the fighter walked in without deciding what the meal is for. There are two decisions that matter.

**Decision one: is this a fueling meal or a maintenance meal?** A fueling meal is a meal that has to feed a training session, either recently completed or upcoming inside the next 12 hours. A maintenance meal is anywhere else in the week — a social dinner on an off-day, a family lunch on a light day, a road-trip meal between drives. The two have completely different macro shapes. A fueling meal wants meaningful carbohydrate (150 to 250 grams depending on body mass and session load), 40 to 60 grams of protein, and modest fat. A maintenance meal wants protein-forward (40 to 50 grams), moderate carbohydrate (60 to 100 grams), and controlled fat. Confusing them is where most fighters lose the plot — they eat a fueling-shaped meal on a maintenance day and take on 800 unnecessary calories in carbs and fat.

**Decision two: what is the calorie ceiling for this meal?** Before you walk in, you should know the number. If your daily target is 3200 kcal and you have already logged 1400 across breakfast and a mid-day snack, this dinner has a 1200 kcal ceiling with a 300 kcal buffer for tomorrow's morning weigh-in check. That is the number you decide the meal against. Not "I will just eat clean." Not "I will just have less than usual." A number. Fighters who go into a restaurant without the number are fighters who leave the restaurant surprised.

Make both decisions in the car before you walk in. It takes 30 seconds. It is the single highest-leverage habit in restaurant navigation.

## The 45-Second Menu Read

Once you sit down, the menu read is a mechanical routine. Run it every time until it is automatic.

**Step one — scan for the protein anchor.** Find the highest-quality, leanest protein on the menu that you would eat. Grilled chicken, grilled fish, sirloin, flat iron, shrimp, chicken breast. Ignore fried anything, breaded anything, and cream-sauce anything. If the protein anchor does not exist on the menu, you are in the wrong restaurant for a camp meal — leave and go elsewhere, or accept the meal will be a controlled off-target.

**Step two — scan for the carbohydrate source you can quantify.** Rice, potato, sweet potato, pasta, corn tortilla, whole-grain bread. You want a carbohydrate you can eyeball a serving of. Ambiguous grain mixes, "ancient grain blend," "quinoa medley" — these are harder to portion. Simple is better because you have to eyeball it.

**Step three — flag the calorie amplifiers.** Every restaurant plate has three or four amplifiers that account for 60 to 80 percent of the calorie load you did not expect. In order of frequency: dressing, oil (both cooking oil on the protein and finishing oil on vegetables), cheese, and sauces (BBQ, teriyaki, buffalo, ranch, honey mustard). The chicken breast is 200 kcal. The dressing on the side salad is 400. The oil the kitchen sautéed the vegetables in is 200. The bread service is 300. The chicken breast is not the problem.

**Step four — build the plate in your head before ordering.** Protein anchor at the target grams. Carbohydrate at the target grams. Vegetables. Dressings and sauces on the side, requested by name at the order. Cooking modifications you can ask for: "grilled instead of fried," "no butter on the vegetables," "no oil on the grill for the chicken," "sauce on the side." Say them at the order, not after the plate arrives.

The whole read runs in under a minute once you have practiced it. Most fighters who "cannot eat out during camp" have simply never installed the routine. It is not intuition. It is a checklist.

## The Six Restaurant Categories and Their Traps

Fight-camp restaurant meals fall into six categories that come up over and over. Each has a specific trap and a specific plate build that works.

**Steakhouse.** The trap is the sides. The steak itself is a clean protein at a portion you can quantify (6 to 12 oz depending on how you order). The mashed potatoes are 400 kcal per cup of butter and cream. The creamed spinach is a fat bomb disguised as a vegetable. The bread service alone is 500 kcal before the meal starts. Plate build: sirloin or filet at 6 to 8 oz, plain baked potato with salt and pepper, side of steamed vegetables no butter, skip the bread. This is one of the easier restaurants to survive during camp if you order the sides deliberately.

**Sushi.** The trap is the rice, the sauces, and the deep-fried rolls. A specialty roll can hit 600 to 900 kcal each — most of it refined-carbohydrate rice, mayo-based sauces, and cream cheese. A "healthy" order of two specialty rolls is a 1500 kcal meal that does not look like one. Plate build: sashimi over rolls when you can, nigiri or hand rolls that let you count the pieces, one specialty roll maximum and only from the menu section that lists a non-fried non-mayo option, edamame as the starter instead of tempura, miso soup for sodium if you are pushing fluid. If the restaurant has poke bowls, that is a cleaner option than most rolls — brown rice base, protein, controlled sauce, vegetables.

**Mexican.** The trap is the chip basket and the cheese. Two baskets of chips with salsa is 700 kcal before the entree arrives. Cheese, sour cream, and guacamole layered on a burrito or a bowl are collectively another 400 to 600 kcal. Plate build: send the chip basket back or ask them not to bring one, order fajitas with no sour cream and cheese on the side (or skipped entirely), soft corn tortillas over flour, grilled protein of your choice, black beans and rice at controlled portions, salsa and pico de gallo unlimited. Guacamole is fine — the fat is quality, but the portion is what runs it — 2 tablespoons is a serving, not the whole cup they bring.

**Italian.** The trap is the pasta portion and the bread. Restaurant pasta portions are 2 to 3 servings by weight, and the bread service adds another 400 to 500 kcal. Plate build: grilled chicken parmesan or grilled fish over pasta on the fueling-day version, veal or chicken piccata or marsala with a side of vegetables on the maintenance-day version, ask for pasta portion at half if you want it, skip the bread service, side salad with dressing on the side. Italian is manageable but requires the portion request at the order — the kitchen will bring the standard portion otherwise.

**Diner or breakfast.** The trap is the volume of everything and the free refills of pancake syrup and hash browns. An "American breakfast" plate averages 1400 to 1800 kcal before the toast is buttered. Plate build: egg-white omelet with vegetables and grilled chicken or turkey inside, side of fresh fruit instead of hash browns, one slice of dry whole-wheat toast, black coffee. If it is a fueling meal, add a plain oatmeal on the side. If the diner does not have egg whites, order three whole eggs any style, one slice of turkey bacon, fruit, dry toast, and skip the potato side. Do not touch the pancake or waffle side of the menu during camp.

**Fast-casual bowl (Chipotle, Sweetgreen, Cava, etc.).** The trap is the base portion and the dressing. Every bowl chain builds around a rice or grain base that they scoop three times heavier than a home portion. The dressings and creamy toppings compound. Plate build: half rice base or double green base, double protein (grilled chicken, grilled steak, grilled fish), one bean or legume portion, unlimited vegetables and pico, one modest scoop of creamy element (guac, tzatziki, dressing) instead of two, hard skip the crispy toppings and cheese if the bowl already has creamy element. The rice-base halving alone saves 250 to 350 kcal per bowl.

## The Traps That Cross All Six Categories

A few patterns hit at every category and warrant a dedicated read.

**The pre-meal bread or chip service.** This is 300 to 700 kcal before the meal starts, and it is invisible to most fighters because it is "just the free thing before the meal." Ask the server not to bring it. If it is already on the table, push it to the far end of the table.

**Beverage calories.** A single 20 oz sweetened beverage — regular soda, sweet tea, agua fresca, a large juice — is 200 to 300 kcal. A pint of beer is another 200. A cocktail is 200 to 400. During camp, order water with lemon or unsweetened tea. Beverages are the single easiest calorie cut in the restaurant setting and the one fighters most often overlook.

**The "healthy" branding trap.** Salads labeled with words like "chopped," "harvest," "power," or "Cobb" are among the highest-calorie items on many menus because they are 60 percent dressing, cheese, bacon, croutons, and dried fruit by calorie share. A restaurant chopped salad can hit 1100 kcal easily. If you are ordering a salad as your entree, order it with grilled chicken or grilled fish, dressing on the side, half the cheese, no croutons or dried fruit. Ordering "just a salad" is not the same as ordering a low-calorie meal.

**The shared appetizer.** Two shared appetizers between four people is 200 to 400 kcal per person before the entree. If the group is ordering appetizers, take a small portion, not a proportional share, or opt out.

## The Recovery Protocol When You Overshoot

Sometimes the meal runs over. The kitchen doubled the pasta portion. The chef pre-sauced the dish. The team took you somewhere you did not choose. When the meal runs 500 to 1000 kcal over the ceiling, run the recovery protocol.

**The next morning.** Weigh in first thing. Do not skip a weigh-in because you know it will be up — the number is the data, not the verdict. Expect 1 to 3 pounds of water and gastric weight above the trend line. This is not fat gain. It reads correctly by 48 to 72 hours if the following days are on target.

**The next 24 hours.** Reduce the day's calorie target by 400 to 500 kcal, not the full 500 to 1000 you overshot. Cutting the full overage in one day disrupts fueling and creates a compensatory hunger swing that leads to a second overshoot on day three. Take the correction across 2 to 3 days.

**Sodium and fluid.** A restaurant meal often carries 3000 to 5000 mg of sodium, which drives short-term water retention. Increase plain water intake by 500 to 750 mL over the next day. Do not overdrink — pushing 2 to 3 liters extra in a panic response is its own problem. Steady increase, spread across the day.

**Training the next day.** Do not add a compensatory session or extra cardio to "burn it off." The training-load plan is set for a reason. A one-meal overshoot is metabolically trivial across a camp; adding an unplanned session risks a real injury or an overtraining hit that costs more than the meal did.

**The camp-level lesson.** Every off-target meal is data. What triggered the overshoot? Was it decision-fatigue late in the day? A restaurant category you had not planned for? A social dynamic you did not manage? Write it down. Fighters who make weight across multiple camps are the fighters who convert every off-target meal into a rule for the next camp.

## The Coaching Read

Restaurant navigation is one of the two or three habits that separates fighters who make weight easily from fighters who suffer through every camp. The mechanical parts — the 45-second menu read, the six-category plate builds, the beverage rule — are simple to learn. The harder part is the decision to run the routine every time, not just when you feel like it.

A camp is a series of decisions, not a plan. The fighter who eats out three times a week during camp and runs the menu read every time is going to make weight cleaner than the fighter who eats out once a month and treats it as a free meal. Consistency of the routine matters more than frequency of the meals. Install it early in camp, use it on the low-stakes meals, and by the time the fight-week team dinner arrives you are not deciding anything — you are just running the routine.

Weight is made in the kitchen 80 percent of the time. The other 20 percent is made at the restaurant table with the menu open and a fork in your hand. Both count.
