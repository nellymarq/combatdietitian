Fight week is where the sleep-quality-and-nutrition wheels come off in a majority of the fighters I work with, and the reflex fix — a 5 or 10 mg melatonin capsule at bedtime — is the wrong tool for the job. Melatonin is a sleep-onset timing signal, not a sleep-depth or sleep-continuity tool. Dosing it at 10 to 20 times the physiologic level for three nights running frequently backfires as fragmented sleep, morning-of-weigh-in grogginess, and blunted appetite regulation that shows up as a food-refusal window during the exact rehydration and refuel period the fighter cannot afford to lose. The specific sleep failures fight-week fighters run into — a sympathetic nervous system that will not stand down, a sleep-onset latency that stretches from 15 to 60-plus minutes as bedtime creeps later, a reduction in slow-wave sleep depth as the weight cut deepens, and a core-body-temperature curve flattened by dehydration and hot-cutting protocols — are not melatonin-shaped problems.

Magnesium and glycine are the two under-used sleep-nutrition tools that target exactly the physiology that fails in fight week. They are cheap, safe, well-tolerated in the doses that work, and have a dose-response window that stacks with the carb-plus-tryptophan meal structure the fighter is already running. Used correctly across the three nights before weigh-ins, they deliver more of the sleep architecture that actually recovers a fighter than any melatonin protocol does. Used incorrectly — wrong form, wrong timing, wrong dose relative to the meal — they do essentially nothing.

This is the timing-and-dosing playbook.

## Why the Melatonin Reflex Is the Wrong Answer

Endogenous melatonin production peaks at roughly 0.3 mg per night distributed across the sleep window, with plasma concentrations that rise from evening baseline through a peak around 2 to 4 a.m. and then decline. The purpose of the melatonin signal is timing — it tells the sleep-wake system when biological night has arrived and when to release the wake drive. Melatonin is not a hypnotic. It does not deepen sleep, does not consolidate sleep across the second half of the night, and does not modulate the sympathetic-nervous-system tone that is the actual barrier to sleep onset in the anxious, dehydrated, cutting fighter.

The retail supplement industry standardized on 3, 5, and 10 mg capsules — 10 to 30 times the endogenous peak — and dosing at that level for three nights running produces two problems in fight-week specifically. First, the exogenous melatonin loads suppress the endogenous rhythm, and the third or fourth night of dosing frequently produces the paradoxical pattern where the fighter falls asleep quickly, wakes at 2 or 3 a.m., and cannot get back down. Second, morning melatonin clearance is slow at the megadose level, and the residual plasma melatonin at the 6 a.m. weigh-in wake-up is what drives the grogginess, blunted appetite, and reduced rehydration compliance that show up in the two-hour window after weigh-ins.

If melatonin has a place in fight week at all, it is a 0.3 to 0.5 mg dose taken 90 minutes before target bedtime as a timing signal for a travel-related circadian phase shift — not the 5 to 10 mg bedtime reflex.

## Magnesium — the Sympathetic-Nervous-System Down-Regulator

Magnesium is a cofactor in over 300 enzymatic reactions in the body, and its role in the sleep-nutrition conversation is the specific effect on the NMDA-receptor and GABA-receptor signaling that governs sympathetic tone and sleep-onset latency. In magnesium-adequate athletes, the effect on sleep parameters is modest. In magnesium-inadequate athletes — a category most cutting fighters fall into by fight week because of sweat-mediated magnesium losses, low-fiber weight-cut diets that skip the magnesium-carrying leafy greens and legumes, and diuretic-associated urinary magnesium losses — the corrective effect on sleep-onset latency and sleep continuity is meaningful.

**Form matters.** Magnesium oxide (the cheapest and most common retail form) has an absorption fraction of roughly 4 percent and is essentially an osmotic laxative at any effective dose. Magnesium citrate absorbs at roughly 25 to 30 percent and is a reasonable second-tier choice but carries a meaningful GI-loosening effect at the doses required for the sleep signal. Magnesium bisglycinate (also labeled glycinate) is the form the sleep-nutrition literature has settled on: 30-plus percent absorption, minimal GI effect, and the glycine amino-acid carrier itself contributes to the sleep pathway I'll cover in the next section. Magnesium threonate has some brain-penetration data behind it for cognitive endpoints but is roughly 5 times the cost per elemental milligram and does not carry a meaningfully better sleep-endpoint signal than glycinate.

**Dose.** The studied range for sleep-onset-latency and sleep-quality endpoints in adult subjects sits at 200 to 400 mg elemental magnesium per night, taken 45 to 90 minutes before target bedtime. For the fight-week fighter, the practical dose is 300 mg elemental magnesium as bisglycinate, taken with a small evening carbohydrate serving 60 to 90 minutes before bed. A good option is Scythene's [Magnesium Bisglycinate](https://scythene.com/products/magnesium-bisglycinate?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=fight-week-sleep-nutrition-magnesium-glycine-timing-playbook), which discloses the elemental magnesium content per serving instead of hiding it inside a "magnesium bisglycinate 1000 mg" claim that turns out to be 100 mg elemental once the glycine carrier weight is stripped out.

**Timing.** The magnesium sleep signal is a slow-onset effect. Dosing at bedtime — the reflex most fighters default to — puts the peak plasma concentration in the middle of the second sleep cycle rather than at the sleep-onset window where the sympathetic-tone effect is needed. The 60-to-90-minute pre-bed window is the correct anchor.

**Contraindications and interactions.** Fighters on any prescription medication should check magnesium against their prescription — magnesium can reduce absorption of some antibiotics (tetracyclines, quinolones) and thyroid medications and should be spaced by 4-plus hours. Fighters with any renal impairment should not run the sleep-dose magnesium protocol without primary-care clearance.

## Glycine — the Core-Body-Temperature-Drop Facilitator

Glycine is a non-essential amino acid that operates as an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system. Its role in the sleep-nutrition conversation is the specific effect on the peripheral-vasodilation-mediated core-body-temperature drop that gates the transition from wake into deep sleep. The sleep architecture literature has repeatedly demonstrated that the core-body-temperature drop across sleep onset — roughly 0.5 to 1.0 degrees Celsius from evening baseline — is a prerequisite for the slow-wave sleep depth that carries most of the recovery signal, and glycine dosed at 3 grams pre-bed reproducibly accelerates that temperature-drop window.

**Dose.** The Kawai et al. and Yamadera et al. trials that anchor the glycine-sleep literature both used 3 grams glycine taken 60 minutes before target bedtime, producing subjective sleep-quality improvements, reduced sleep-onset latency, and improved next-day subjective daytime function. The dose response is fairly flat above 3 grams — 5 grams does not clearly outperform 3 grams — and the effect is reproducible across single-night and multi-night protocols.

**Form and administration.** Glycine is a small, sweet-tasting amino acid available as an inexpensive bulk powder. Three grams dissolved in 4 to 6 ounces of water is the practical delivery. Capsules work but the 500 mg to 1 gram per capsule sizing means the fighter is swallowing 3 to 6 capsules per dose, which is friction most fighters skip on the second or third night.

**Timing.** The 60-minute pre-bed window matches the peripheral-vasodilation-and-core-temperature-drop timeline. Dosing at bedtime works less well; dosing 2 hours before bed misses the temperature window and the signal is blunted.

**Stacking with magnesium.** Magnesium bisglycinate delivers roughly 700 to 800 mg of glycine per 300 mg elemental magnesium serving via the glycine carrier weight. That is not the full 3-gram glycine dose the sleep signal is anchored to, but it does provide the roughly 25 percent contribution and reduces the standalone glycine dose to 2 to 2.5 grams if the fighter is stacking both. In practical protocols I run the two separately at the same 60-to-90-minute pre-bed window so the doses are transparent and the fighter can adjust either lever independently.

## The Carbohydrate-Plus-Tryptophan Meal Structure

The nutrition side of the sleep signal is not just the supplement pair. The evening meal structure that supports the magnesium-glycine protocol is a moderate-to-high carbohydrate serving with a tryptophan-carrying protein source, timed 2 to 3 hours before target bedtime. The mechanism is straightforward: carbohydrate consumption raises the tryptophan-to-large-neutral-amino-acid ratio at the blood-brain barrier, tryptophan crosses into the CNS more effectively, and the serotonin-then-melatonin biosynthesis pathway runs on the substrate the meal delivered.

Practical fight-week evening meals that carry this structure: rice-and-chicken bowl with a fruit serving, oatmeal-and-honey with a scoop of whey protein, a sweet potato with turkey and a green vegetable, or a pasta-and-lean-meat plate with a small dessert. The fighter running a hot-cut or a food-restriction final 48 hours will not be eating these meals on nights two and three; the supplement pair carries more of the load on those nights, and the pre-cut nights one and two are where the meal structure earns its slot.

**The tryptophan-source hierarchy for fight-week fighters:** turkey and chicken (highest tryptophan-to-body-weight ratio per serving), whey protein (concentrated tryptophan availability), eggs (moderate tryptophan, easy to prepare), and dairy (variable but generally moderate). The Instagram tryptophan-warm-milk-at-bedtime story is directionally correct on the biochemistry but the dose per serving is small — a cup of warm milk delivers roughly 150 mg tryptophan against a 500-plus-mg dietary-day intake target, and it is doing much less than the carbohydrate-plus-larger-protein-meal 2 to 3 hours earlier.

## The Caffeine-Clearance Failure That Undoes Everything

Caffeine has a half-life of 5 to 7 hours in the average adult metabolizer and up to 10-plus hours in the slow-metabolizer genotype (CYP1A2 slow variant). A 200 mg pre-workout dose taken at 2 p.m. leaves 100 mg on board at 8 p.m. and 50 mg on board at 1 a.m. — a plasma level that meaningfully suppresses adenosine receptor availability and blunts the deep-sleep signal the magnesium-glycine protocol is trying to build.

The fight-week caffeine curve I hold with fighters:

- **Three nights before weigh-ins:** last caffeine dose no later than 12 p.m. If the fighter is running the pre-fight-day caffeine tolerance reset (a distinct protocol), start the taper here.
- **Two nights before weigh-ins:** last caffeine dose no later than 10 a.m., and ideally no caffeine at all for CYP1A2 slow metabolizers.
- **Night before weigh-ins:** zero caffeine after breakfast if any, and preferably no caffeine at all. The sleep signal on this night is the most valuable of the week and the smallest caffeine load will meaningfully degrade it.

Fighters who run the tolerance-reset protocol into fight day (the intentional 4-to-7-day caffeine washout to restore receptor sensitivity for the fight-day dose) are already in the correct position for the sleep protocol. Fighters who continue habitual coffee consumption through fight week are not, and the sleep-nutrition protocol will underdeliver until the caffeine timeline is fixed.

## The Three-Night Protocol

**Night three before weigh-ins (T-3).** Evening meal 2 to 3 hours before bed with rice or oatmeal or sweet potato plus a tryptophan-carrying protein source and a green vegetable. 300 mg elemental magnesium as bisglycinate at 60 to 90 minutes pre-bed. 3 grams glycine dissolved in water at 60 minutes pre-bed. Room temperature at 65 to 68 degrees F to support the core-body-temperature drop. Screens off 45 minutes pre-bed if fighter tolerates the discipline. No caffeine after 12 p.m.

**Night two before weigh-ins (T-2).** Same protocol as T-3 if the weight cut allows the evening meal. If the fighter is in a hot-cut or food-restriction window, hold the supplement pair at the same timing but let the meal structure slide to what the cut allows. This is often the night where sleep is thinnest and the supplement contribution earns most of its weight. No caffeine after 10 a.m.

**Night one before weigh-ins (T-1, the night before weigh-in).** The most important sleep night of the week and the hardest to protect. Depending on the depth of the cut, the fighter may be at meaningful dehydration, may be pre-sauna or post-sauna depending on the cut sequence, and may have a 5 or 6 a.m. weigh-in wake-up ahead of them. The supplement pair still runs at the same timing. If the fighter is in a hot-cut where any pre-bed liquid load is contraindicated, the glycine dose can be delivered in a smaller volume of water (2 ounces instead of 6) or as capsules; the magnesium bisglycinate at 300 mg elemental is roughly 4 capsules with no meaningful fluid load. No caffeine at all.

**Weigh-in morning.** The rehydration and refuel protocol takes over from the sleep protocol here. The magnesium-glycine pair does not carry into the post-weigh-in window and should not be re-dosed until the fight-night sleep planning if a two-day fight-week is in play.

## The Two Mistakes That Wreck the Protocol

**Melatonin megadosing on top of the magnesium-glycine pair.** The reflex to add "everything that might help" produces the paradoxical fragmentation and morning grogginess I described in the opening. Pick the correct tool for the correct problem. The magnesium-glycine pair addresses the sympathetic-tone, sleep-onset-latency, and slow-wave-depth failures that dominate fight week. If a circadian phase shift is actually in play (a fighter who flew 4-plus time zones for the fight and needs to shift bedtime), a 0.3 to 0.5 mg melatonin timing dose 90 minutes before target bedtime is defensible. If there is no phase shift, no melatonin.

**Pre-bed caffeine that the fighter forgot was caffeine.** Dark chocolate at 20 to 40 mg caffeine per ounce, pre-workout supplements taken for an evening drilling session, some green teas at 30 to 50 mg per cup, and the "cold and flu" over-the-counter medications that carry caffeine as an active ingredient — all of these show up as failed sleep protocols where the fighter swears they had "no caffeine after noon." Run the audit at the start of fight week and pull every caffeine source the fighter has not accounted for.

## Cornerman and Coach Checklist

Print or memorize this list and run it at the start of every fight-week:

- Magnesium bisglycinate 300 mg elemental, 60 to 90 min pre-bed, three nights running
- Glycine 3 g in water, 60 min pre-bed, three nights running
- Last caffeine dose: T-3 at 12 p.m., T-2 at 10 a.m., T-1 zero
- Evening meal 2 to 3 hours pre-bed with rice/oats/sweet potato + turkey/chicken/eggs/whey + green vegetable (nights permit)
- Room temperature 65 to 68 F, screens off 45 min pre-bed
- No melatonin unless a real circadian phase shift is in play, and if so 0.3 to 0.5 mg only

The fight is won or lost across the fight-week sleep window more often than most fighters realize. Get the sleep protocol right and the fighter walks to the ring with a nervous system that stood down, a slow-wave sleep debt paid down, and a rehydration compliance the following morning that the melatonin-reflex fighter will not have.
