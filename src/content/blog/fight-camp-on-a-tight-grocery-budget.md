Most fight-camp nutrition writing has an unspoken assumption sitting underneath it: the fighter has a personal chef, an unlimited grocery budget, a $400/month supplement stack, and access to a chest freezer full of grass-fed ribeye. That fighter exists. Most amateurs do not live in that world.

The world most amateurs actually live in: a full-time job, a $150-to-$200 weekly grocery budget for the whole household, one or two teammates helping split a bulk-meat order, a shared apartment kitchen, and a body that still needs 3,200 calories a day and 2 grams of protein per kilogram to survive a two-a-day camp. The math is not comfortable, but it is workable — and the fighters winning amateur titles on tight budgets are the ones who understand the per-dollar hierarchy of protein, carbohydrate, and micronutrient sources cold.

This is the ground-truth playbook. What to buy, what to skip, how to prep it, and where to hold the line when money is the constraint.

## The Per-Dollar Protein Hierarchy

The single biggest lever on the amateur budget is the protein source ranking. Most fighters buy protein in the wrong order — chicken breast and lean ground beef first because that is what shows up in every meal plan they have ever read, and then they run out of grocery money by Thursday. The per-gram-of-protein-per-dollar hierarchy at typical U.S. supermarket pricing (2026) reorders the shopping list cleanly:

- **Whole eggs.** Roughly 6 g protein per egg, $0.20-$0.30 per egg. That is $0.03-$0.05 per gram of protein — the cheapest complete-protein source on the shelf, with a full amino-acid profile, choline for brain health, and the vitamin D + K2 the fighter is probably underdosed on. **Buy 2-3 dozen a week.** The old cholesterol worry has not held up in the last decade of controlled trials for otherwise healthy adults.
- **Canned tuna and canned sardines.** 22 g protein per 5-oz can of tuna at $1.00-$1.50 per can, or 20 g protein per 3.75-oz can of sardines at $1.50-$2.50 per can. That is $0.05-$0.07 per gram of protein from tuna and $0.08-$0.12 from sardines. Sardines are the higher-value buy on the omega-3 side — one can delivers 800-1,200 mg EPA + DHA, which is a supplemental-grade dose from real food.
- **Dry beans and lentils.** 15 g protein per cooked cup at a raw cost of roughly $0.20-$0.40 per cooked cup. That is $0.02-$0.03 per gram — the cheapest protein on the list, though not a complete amino-acid profile on its own. Paired with rice, wheat, or corn, the profile becomes complete. **The 1-lb bag of dry black beans at $1.50 is the amateur fighter's secret weapon.**
- **Whole chicken (not breast).** A 5-lb whole chicken at $1.20-$1.80/lb yields roughly 2.5 lb of usable cooked meat with 25 g protein per 4-oz portion. That works out to $0.07-$0.10 per gram of protein. **The whole bird is 40-60% cheaper per gram of protein than boneless-skinless chicken breast**, and the carcass makes 2-3 quarts of stock for free.
- **Ground beef 80/20.** 22 g protein per 4-oz cooked portion at $4-$6/lb. That is $0.10-$0.15 per gram of protein. Fine as a rotation protein but not the workhorse.
- **[Whey protein powder](https://scythene.com/products/whey-protein-vanilla?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=fight-camp-on-a-tight-grocery-budget).** 25 g protein per scoop at $0.75-$1.20 per scoop from a bulk 5-lb tub. That is $0.03-$0.05 per gram — competitive with eggs on unit cost, and the highest leucine-per-serving option on the list for the post-training MPS trigger.
- **Chicken breast (boneless-skinless).** 26 g protein per 4-oz cooked at $4.50-$7/lb. That is $0.15-$0.20 per gram of protein. **This is where most amateur fighters overspend.** Rotate it in, do not build the camp around it.
- **Sirloin, ribeye, salmon fillet.** $0.20-$0.40 per gram of protein at $9-$15/lb. Special-occasion meals or reward meals, not the daily build.

The rotation that hits 180 g protein per day on a 90-kg fighter without breaking the budget: 4 whole eggs at breakfast (24 g), 1 can of tuna at lunch (22 g), 6 oz of whole-chicken cooked meat at dinner (42 g), 1.5 cups of black beans distributed across the day (22 g), 2 scoops of whey around training (50 g), and 20-30 g from incidental sources (Greek yogurt, cheese, incidental meat in rice-bean bowls). Total: 180-200 g protein per day at a raw ingredient cost of $5-$7 per day.

## The Per-Dollar Carbohydrate Hierarchy

Carbs are where amateur fighters get robbed by "clean eating" marketing that pushes them toward premium ancient grains at three to five times the cost of the staples that actually fuel training. The per-gram-of-carb-per-dollar ranking:

- **White rice (bulk).** A 20-lb bag of long-grain white rice at $18-$25 yields roughly 120 cups of cooked rice at 45 g carbs per cup. That is $0.003-$0.005 per gram of carb — the cheapest fight-camp fuel on the shelf. **White rice is not less nutritious than brown rice for the trained athlete under a heavy training load** — the fiber load of brown rice can slow gastric emptying pre-training, and the micronutrient gap is filled easily by the vegetables in the rest of the diet.
- **Rolled oats.** A 42-oz canister at $4-$6 yields roughly 40 servings at 27 g carbs per half-cup dry. That is $0.005-$0.008 per gram of carb. **The bulk oats bin at any warehouse club is under $0.90/lb** — the cheapest hot breakfast in existence.
- **Potatoes (russet, sweet, yukon gold).** $0.60-$1.20/lb at 20-30 g carbs per medium potato. That is $0.02-$0.04 per gram of carb. Fills volume, holds well in the fridge, and roasts in 40 minutes with almost no attention.
- **Pasta (dry).** $1-$2 per lb-box yielding 45 g carbs per 2-oz dry serving. That is $0.005-$0.010 per gram of carb. The 24-oz jar of tomato sauce at $2-$3 pushes the whole-meal cost under $2 for four servings.
- **Bananas.** $0.20-$0.35 per banana at 27 g carbs each. That is $0.007-$0.013 per gram of carb — the cheapest pre-training fast carb on the shelf, portable, no prep. **Buy a bunch every grocery trip.**
- **Corn tortillas.** A 30-count pack at $2-$3 yields 12 g carbs per tortilla at roughly $0.08 per tortilla. That is $0.005-$0.008 per gram of carb.
- **Frozen berries (bulk).** A 3-lb bag of mixed berries at $10-$14 yields roughly 30 half-cup servings at 8-10 g carbs each. That is $0.04-$0.06 per gram of carb — expensive per-carb but bought for the antioxidant load and the flavor lever, not the fuel.

**Skip:** ancient grains sold at $6-$10/lb, quinoa at $6-$8/lb (a nutrition-marketing artifact — it is not meaningfully better than rice for a fighter's needs), pre-cooked microwave rice pouches at 4-5x the price of bulk rice, and anything labeled "keto" during a fight camp.

Total carb intake at 400-500 g/day on training-heavy days from the staples above lands at $2-$4 per day in raw ingredient cost.

## The Micronutrient Anchors

Protein and carbs get the airtime, but the micronutrient anchors are where most budget diets fail — a fighter can hit the macros perfectly and still be underfed on iron, magnesium, potassium, and vitamin K if the vegetable and fruit slots collapse. The unit-cost-stable anchors:

- **Frozen spinach.** A 1-lb bag at $1.50-$2.50 lasts a week and delivers iron, magnesium, folate, and vitamin K1 that no other cheap green matches. Cook it into the eggs, into the rice bowl, into anything.
- **Bagged carrots.** A 5-lb bag at $4-$6 delivers beta-carotene, potassium, and fiber for weeks. Roast a sheet pan on Sunday.
- **Cabbage or coleslaw mix.** A head of green cabbage at $2-$4 or a bagged coleslaw at $2-$3 delivers vitamin K, vitamin C, and volume for salads that fill the plate without a $10 mixed-greens clamshell.
- **Onions and garlic.** $1-$2/lb for onions, $0.50-$1 per garlic head. The flavor foundation of every home-cooked meal — the fighter who can cook onions and garlic has half of world cuisine at their fingertips.
- **Frozen broccoli or frozen mixed vegetables.** $2-$4 per 1-lb bag. Steam-in-bag, four minutes in the microwave, done. No trimming, no chopping, no waste.
- **Canned tomatoes.** $1-$2 per can. The base of every stew, chili, and pasta sauce. Full-fat coconut milk at $2-$3/can rounds out any curry-style base.
- **Bananas + apples + oranges.** The three shelf-stable, portable, ready-to-eat fruits under $1 per piece. **Buy enough for two per day.**

The $30-$40/week produce budget above delivers the vitamin C, potassium, magnesium, folate, and vitamin K a fighter's connective tissue and neuromuscular system depend on — without the $80/week Whole Foods produce bill.

## The Supplement Discipline When Money Is Tight

The supplement industry is designed to extract money from ambitious amateurs. The honest ranking of what actually earns its slot in the amateur fighter's budget:

- **[Creatine monohydrate](https://scythene.com/products/creatine-monohydrate?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=fight-camp-on-a-tight-grocery-budget).** $15-$20 for a 500-g tub that lasts 4-5 months at 3-5 g/day. Best-studied performance supplement in sports, safe, cheap, real. **Non-negotiable if the money exists at all.**
- **[Electrolytes](https://scythene.com/products/electrolytes-berry?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=fight-camp-on-a-tight-grocery-budget) for hard sessions and weight-cut work.** $25-$35 for a 30-serving tub. **Actually needed** during two-a-days and any sauna work.
- **Vitamin D3 (2,000-5,000 IU/day).** $8-$15 for a 6-month supply. Almost every fighter training in a windowless gym in a northern climate is underdosed on D. Cheap fix.
- **Whey protein.** Not a supplement in the strict sense — it is a bulk food source that happens to come in a tub. Earns its slot on cost-per-gram-protein grounds. See section above.

**What to skip when the budget is tight:** pre-workout blends (caffeine from black coffee or tea does the same work at 5% of the cost), BCAAs (if protein is adequate, BCAAs add nothing), fat burners, testosterone boosters, "recovery formulas" that are just multivitamins with a protein spike, and anything marketed with the word "elite" or "ultimate" on the label.

The total defensible supplement budget for an amateur fighter is $30-$50/month if all three earn their slot. Anything above that is category creep.

## The Sunday Prep Sequence

Cooking every meal from scratch every day is the failure mode for the amateur fighter working a full-time job. The Sunday prep sequence that keeps the whole week fed:

1. **Rice cooker on first thing.** 4 cups of dry white rice → 12 cups of cooked rice → 8 meal portions. 45 minutes, hands-off.
2. **Sheet-pan chicken thighs.** 3-5 lb of bone-in thighs, salt-and-pepper-and-paprika, 400°F for 40 minutes. Pulls apart cleanly, holds all week. Save the drippings.
3. **Big pot of beans.** 1 lb of dry black beans in the slow cooker overnight Saturday, or 90 minutes in a pressure cooker Sunday morning. 6-8 meal portions.
4. **Whole roast chicken.** If not doing the thighs, roast a whole bird — 1 hour 15 minutes at 400°F, breast down for the first 45 minutes. Carcass into the pot Monday morning for stock.
5. **Sheet-pan roasted vegetables.** Whatever is cheapest that week — sweet potato cubes, carrots, cabbage wedges, onion halves. 45 minutes at 425°F.
6. **Hard-boiled eggs.** 12 eggs, one pot of boiling water, 10 minutes. Peel and refrigerate. Portable protein for the truck, the desk drawer, the gym bag.
7. **Overnight oats jars.** 5 mason jars, half-cup oats + cup of milk + banana + tablespoon of peanut butter + pinch of salt in each. Grab-and-go breakfast for the whole workweek.

Total active kitchen time on Sunday: 90 minutes to 2 hours. Total food output: 25-30 individual meal-equivalents ready to grab and eat, plus stock, plus snacks. **The fighter who prepares Sunday afternoon eats correctly all week; the fighter who does not, does not.**

## What This Actually Looks Like on a $180 Week

A representative amateur fighter's shopping list at typical U.S. supermarket 2026 pricing:

- 3 dozen eggs — $12
- 4 cans tuna + 4 cans sardines — $14
- 2 whole chickens (5 lb each) — $16
- 1 lb ground beef — $6
- 1 lb dry black beans + 1 lb dry lentils — $4
- 20-lb bag of rice (buys every 8-10 weeks; amortized $2-$3/wk)
- 42-oz oats — $5
- 8 lb potatoes + 5 lb carrots + 2 heads cabbage — $10
- 2 lb frozen spinach + 2 lb frozen broccoli + 3 lb frozen berries — $16
- 3 lb bananas + 6 apples + 6 oranges — $10
- 4 cans tomatoes + 2 jars pasta sauce + 2 lb dry pasta — $10
- 1 lb onions + 3 garlic heads + spices as needed — $6
- 2 gallons milk + 1 tub Greek yogurt + 1 lb cheddar — $18
- Bulk peanut butter (5 lb) — amortized $3-$4/wk
- Coffee (rotating drip grinds) — $8/wk

Running total: $155-$185/week. Delivers 3,000-3,500 kcal/day, 180-200 g protein/day, 400-500 g carbs/day, 30-40 g fiber/day, and the full micronutrient stack a fighter needs. Add $8-$12/week for the amortized creatine and vitamin D, and the whole thing lands under $200/week.

## Where to Hold the Line

Two lines cannot be crossed even when the budget is tightest:

**Protein does not drop below 1.6 g/kg body weight per day**, no matter how tight the week gets. If a fighter has to eat rice and beans and eggs for three days straight to hold that number, that is what the week looks like. Under-fueling protein during a fight camp is not a budget decision — it is a decision to arrive at fight night undermuscled.

**Post-training feeding is not optional.** The 30-to-60-minute window after hard training is when the body is most primed to rebuild what the session broke down. A boiled egg + a banana + a glass of milk on the ride home from the gym costs $1 and does the work of a $40 recovery-drink stack. **The fighter who trains hard and then does not eat within the hour is throwing the session away.**

Every other line — supplement stack, organic vs. conventional produce, grass-fed vs. commodity beef, protein powder brand, hydration-drink brand — is negotiable when money is the constraint. The two lines above are not.

## The Coach's Read

Amateur fighters do not lose to nutrition because they cannot afford salmon and ancient grains. They lose to nutrition because they buy the wrong things in the wrong order, run out of grocery money on Wednesday, eat gas-station food for three days, and show up to Thursday's hard sparring undermuscled and unfueled. The fix is not more money — the fix is buying eggs and rice and beans and whole chickens in that order, prepping Sunday, and holding the two lines above.

A coach who wants to protect an amateur fighter's development on a tight budget teaches this playbook once at the start of camp, spot-checks the fridge photo the fighter sends every Sunday afternoon, and closes the loop when the fighter is short on the money for a supplement stack that would not have earned its slot anyway. The amateurs winning titles are eating out of the same grocery stores as the amateurs losing them. What separates them is the shopping list.
