The food log tells you what you ate. It does not tell you what happened to your blood sugar after you ate it, or what happened during the two-hour tempo run you did at 6 AM on an empty stomach, or what your blood sugar did at 2 AM after a hard Monday sparring session that you went to bed ravenous after. A $50 continuous glucose monitor — a disposable sensor the size of a quarter that sticks to the back of your arm with an adhesive tab and streams your blood sugar to your phone every 15 minutes for 10 to 14 days — tells you all of that. For about the cost of a single supplement run, it tells you more about how your fueling is actually working during fight camp than every macro-tracking app you have ever used.

CGMs are not a tool for diagnosing diabetes in combat athletes. The vast majority of fighters are metabolically healthy, and a one-time 10-day readout is not a clinical test for anything. What the CGM is good for — extremely good for — is catching the five or six specific fueling patterns that reliably derail hard fight camps and that the food log will never surface because the food log cannot see what the body does with the food.

This is the fight-camp CGM protocol. What to wear, when to wear it, the five patterns to look for, and how to use the data without letting it talk you into something stupid.

## What the CGM Actually Measures

The CGM does not measure blood glucose directly. It measures interstitial fluid glucose — the sugar in the fluid between your skin cells — through a tiny filament that sits a few millimeters under the skin surface. Interstitial glucose lags blood glucose by about 5 to 15 minutes, which matters less than you think for the patterns we care about because we are looking at hours-long trends, not single readings.

The commercially available over-the-counter options as of 2026 are the Abbott Libre family (Libre 3, Libre Rio) and the Dexcom Stelo — both FDA-cleared for non-diabetic consumer use, both paired with a free phone app, both roughly $50 to $90 for a single 10-to-14-day sensor. Stick it on the back of your upper arm (not over a muscle you load heavily — the biceps-brachii belly is fine, the deltoid is iffy if you throw a lot of hooks), scan or Bluetooth-pair it to your phone, and wear it through camp.

The numbers to anchor against:
- **Fasted morning in a healthy non-diabetic athlete:** 70 to 95 mg/dL
- **Postprandial peak 60 to 120 minutes after a meal:** typically 110 to 160 mg/dL, rarely above 180 mg/dL in a healthy athlete
- **Return to baseline by 180 minutes post-meal:** back below 120 mg/dL
- **Overnight range:** 70 to 110 mg/dL, with the low point usually hitting around 3 to 5 AM
- **Hypoglycemia threshold:** below 70 mg/dL is low, below 54 mg/dL is clinically significant

Those numbers are your reference frame. What makes CGM data valuable for a fighter is not any single reading. It is the pattern across days.

## Pattern One: The Fasted Cardio Hypoglycemic Tail

A fighter runs 60 minutes of zone-two cardio at 6 AM on an empty stomach. They have been doing this for three weeks. They feel fine during the run — the perceived exertion is low, the pace feels sustainable. They stop running, walk to the car, drive home, and feel progressively worse across the next 45 minutes. They are irritable, foggy, shaky, and by the time they eat breakfast at 8 AM they are famished and hammer 1,200 calories in 20 minutes.

The CGM trace catches what happened. The fasted-start glucose sat around 85 mg/dL. The exercise initially pulled glucose up — the liver dumped glycogen to fuel the session, and the 20-minute reading was 102 mg/dL. The middle 30 minutes of the run held steady around 95 mg/dL. The last 10 minutes started to drift down, ending the session at 78 mg/dL. The real damage happened in the 30 minutes after the session ended: muscle insulin sensitivity was now at its post-exercise peak, glucose kept leaking out of the circulation into the muscle to replete glycogen, and the liver's glycogen tank was already drained by the session. By 45 minutes post-session the glucose read 58 mg/dL. That is the hypoglycemic tail. That is the fog, the irritation, and the 1,200-calorie breakfast.

The CGM-driven fix is not "stop doing fasted cardio." For many fighters fasted cardio is a useful tool. The fix is pre-session carbs — 15 to 20 grams of fast-acting carb (a banana, dates, a few gels) 20 minutes before the session, or a post-session carb-protein shake within 15 minutes of finishing. Both options prevent the tail. The CGM run on the next session confirms it.

## Pattern Two: The Post-Sparring Second-Dip

A fighter spars hard on Tuesday from 6 to 7:30 PM. They eat a solid post-session meal — rice, chicken, vegetables — at 8:30 PM. They feel fine. They go to bed at 10:30. They sleep okay, wake up groggy, and Wednesday's morning session is garbage.

The CGM trace shows why. The sparring session pulled glucose up (adrenaline + glycogenolysis) to around 145 mg/dL during the hardest rounds. The post-session meal lifted it further to 160 mg/dL around 9:30 PM. Then the long slow decline started. By 11 PM the glucose was back to 95 mg/dL. By 1 AM it was 78 mg/dL. By 3 AM it was 62 mg/dL and stayed below 70 until around 5:30 AM when the dawn cortisol pulse finally pulled it back up.

That is six and a half hours of overnight hypoglycemia. The fighter never wakes up — the brain pulls just enough glucose out of the muscle and liver to stay functional — but the sleep architecture gets wrecked. Growth hormone pulses are blunted, deep sleep percentage drops, and the next morning the HRV reads 18 percent below baseline despite a completely closed sleep ring.

The CGM-driven fix is a slow-carb-plus-fat snack 30 to 45 minutes before bed on hard sparring days — a bowl of oatmeal with peanut butter, a sweet potato with olive oil, or a mix of Greek yogurt and berries. The protein-carb dinner is not enough on its own because the carbs are cleared too fast against the elevated post-session insulin sensitivity. The slow-release snack holds the glucose above 80 mg/dL across the overnight window and the HRV reads normally the next morning.

## Pattern Three: The Pre-Workout Spike-and-Crash

A fighter has three scoops of pre-workout at 5:30 PM for the 6 PM sparring. The pre-workout has 15 grams of dextrose, 200 mg of caffeine, 3 grams of citrulline, and a "performance blend" including a dose of corn starch. They feel amazing in the first round. Round three they feel dead. Round five they are getting outstruck by partners they usually dominate.

The CGM trace catches the mechanism. The pre-workout dextrose plus the simple-carb filler in the "performance blend" pushed glucose from a 95 mg/dL baseline to 178 mg/dL by 6:10 PM — the first round. The insulin response caught up by round 3, and glucose crashed to 68 mg/dL by 6:45 PM. The last two rounds were sparred in mild hypoglycemia while fighting a caffeine-driven sympathetic load that the body was trying to use to compensate.

The fix is not "cut the pre-workout." Caffeine before sparring is generally fine. The fix is swap the fast-carb pre-workout for a slower-fuel pre-session meal 90 to 120 minutes earlier (a chicken-and-rice bowl, a bagel with nut butter, oatmeal with protein), and take the caffeine solo 30 minutes before. The CGM run on the next sparring session shows glucose holding steady between 100 and 135 mg/dL across all five rounds, and the fighter's round-three-plus performance recovers.

## Pattern Four: The Weight-Cut Glucose Floor

A fighter is in the final 10 days of a 15-pound cut. Daily caloric intake is down from a training-phase 3,200 to a cut-phase 2,000. They are training through it — two sessions a day at reduced volume, lots of sauna and bike work. The fasted morning readings on the CGM start creeping down. Day 1 of the cut phase: fasted 82 mg/dL. Day 5: fasted 74 mg/dL. Day 8: fasted 68 mg/dL. Day 10: fasted 64 mg/dL.

The baseline is dropping because the liver glycogen is drained and the dietary carb intake is not keeping up with the training demand. The fighter feels increasingly flat, tempo work gets harder, and the last three nights of the cut they are waking up at 2 AM wired and unable to fall back asleep (that is the counter-regulatory cortisol-and-adrenaline dump the body uses to pull glucose up when it drops below the hypoglycemic threshold overnight).

The CGM is telling you the fighter has hit the glucose floor. The fix is a recalibration of the carb load — move some of the daily protein allocation toward a pre-sleep complex-carb snack, pull 100 to 200 kcal out of the deficit for the final 72 hours and put them into targeted pre-session carbs, and accept that the final 24 hours might not quite hit the scale target. A fighter crossing the weigh-in line in a glucose-floor state will rehydrate badly, warm up badly, and fight badly. The scale number is not worth the performance cost.

## Pattern Five: The Hidden Overnight Low Masked by "I Feel Fine"

Some fighters tolerate overnight hypoglycemia without obvious symptoms. They sleep through it, wake up feeling fine, and the morning session feels normal. But the CGM data across a 10-day block shows that on 6 of those 10 nights they spent between 90 minutes and 3 hours below 70 mg/dL.

This is the pattern with the largest downstream cost that the fighter would never find without the data. The HRV reads okay, the mood is okay, the morning energy is okay — but the chronic overnight sub-threshold glucose state is driving a chronic cortisol elevation that shows up weeks later as elevated resting heart rate, slow recovery between hard sessions, and the eventual week-7 or week-8 overtraining stall. The CGM is the only tool that catches it in the window where a small fix — the pre-sleep snack — prevents the compounding downstream problem.

## When to Wear the CGM and When Not To

A 10-to-14-day CGM run during the middle third of camp — roughly weeks 4 through 6 of an 8-week camp, or weeks 5 through 7 of a 10-week camp — captures the patterns best. You want enough training volume to reveal the fueling mismatches, but you do not want to be in the final weight-cut taper where the dietary pattern is so compressed that the CGM data is only useful for the cut itself.

Do not wear a CGM during the final 72 hours before weigh-in. The dehydration and the rapid glycogen shifts make the readings erratic and the interpretation unreliable. Pull the sensor on the morning you start the final water-load-and-drop phase, or skip the final 2 to 3 days of a 14-day sensor cycle.

Do not use the CGM to drive a diagnosis. A single glucose reading of 180 mg/dL after a slice of cake is not diabetes. A fasted reading of 68 mg/dL on day 2 of a hard camp is not hypoglycemia as a clinical condition. If the baseline fasted reading sits persistently above 110 mg/dL across a 10-day window, or if the overnight low sits persistently below 55 mg/dL, that is worth bringing to a sports-medicine physician. Everything else is fueling data, not diagnostic data.

Do not use the CGM to drive a "lowest-glucose-wins" mentality. Combat athletes are not endurance runners and are not diabetic. Running your fasted glucose at 68 mg/dL every morning is not a win, it is a chronically under-fueled state that will cost you performance. The goal is a steady, stable glucose trace that bounces predictably around meals and training, with no overnight lows and no post-session crashes.

## The Data-to-Action Checklist

After a 10-day CGM run, pull the daily average overnight low, the lowest reading in the 60 minutes after any training session, and the fasted-morning trend across the 10 days. Then run four checks:

**Overnight low.** If any night has a sub-70 mg/dL reading that lasts more than 60 minutes, add a slow-carb-plus-fat pre-sleep snack on evenings following hard sessions.

**Post-session crash.** If the lowest post-session reading in the first 60 minutes after any training session drops below 70 mg/dL, add 20 to 30 grams of carb within 15 minutes of session end.

**Fasted-morning trend.** If the fasted-morning reading drifts down by more than 10 mg/dL across the 10 days without a planned weight-cut taper driving it, your training-phase carb intake is not matching the training demand — increase the daily carb target by 40 to 60 grams.

**Postprandial peaks.** If any meal reliably drives glucose above 180 mg/dL and does not clear below 120 mg/dL by 180 minutes, the meal composition is too heavy on simple carbs for the amount of protein, fat, and fiber paired with it — rebalance.

A CGM is not a replacement for the food log. It is not a replacement for a coach. It is not a replacement for a sports dietitian who can interpret the data in context. What it is, for the fighter who runs it during the middle third of a hard camp, is the only tool that shows you what your fueling is actually doing to your body between the moment the food goes in and the moment the next session starts. That data is worth the $50.
