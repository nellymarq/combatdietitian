Six weeks into an eight-week camp. Fighter is 4 pounds above weigh-in on schedule. Fight is 14 days out. Tuesday afternoon sparring, he lands wrong on a check-kick defense, feels a pop in the calf, and by evening the swelling is bad enough that the team physician tells him no running, no full sparring, no lower-body plyometrics, and no bag-work below the waist for 5 to 7 days. Modified upper-body work is allowed. Return-to-full-training decision at day 6 pending re-exam.

The cut was on schedule. It is not on schedule anymore. And in the next 24 hours, if the coach and the fighter and the corner do not have a plan, one of two things happens. Either the fighter keeps eating on the training-load calorie budget through the layoff and comes back to training four pounds heavier than the cut plan accounted for. Or the fighter panics, drops calories aggressively during the injury week to preserve the cut trajectory, and shows up at day 7 in a hole the last week of camp cannot dig out of — depleted glycogen, blunted tissue repair, no acceleration, and a fight-week cut that turns into a survival cut.

This is the mid-camp injury interruption pattern I have seen at least a dozen times. It costs more fights than the injury itself does. And it is preventable with a protocol that runs on the day of the injury, holds through the layoff, and reloads correctly on the return-to-training day.

## The 24-Hour Audit

Within 24 hours of the injury and the medical opinion, four things have to be captured:

**What is the exact training-load reduction?** Not "he's out for a few days." Specifically: which sessions are cancelled, which are modified, what the modification looks like. A fighter who can still do upper-body strength, mitts standing-only, and stationary-bike zone-2 conditioning is not on the same calorie budget as a fighter who is on complete rest with an ice-and-elevate protocol. The difference between those two scenarios is 400 to 800 calories per day. Get the exact list from the physician and the coach, in writing where possible, so you are budgeting against the real training load and not against a guess.

**What is the current weight trajectory against the fight-week plan?** Weigh in the morning after the injury, in the same conditions as the previous morning weigh-in. Note the number. Do not react to it yet — that number is going to be inflated by the acute inflammatory response and the fluid shift that follows any soft-tissue injury. The trajectory that matters is the 3-day rolling average, not the single-day number, and the injury day is not a data point you should react to.

**What is the injury type and what does it need nutritionally?** A calf strain, an ankle sprain, a mild MCL, a hyperextended finger, and a broken nose all require different nutritional support. Soft-tissue injuries with muscle involvement need elevated protein and adequate carbohydrate for tissue repair. Bone injuries need calcium, vitamin D, and mechanical loading (which you don't have during the layoff). Skin-and-cartilage injuries need vitamin C and adequate protein. The general rule during any injury layoff is protein UP, carbohydrate calibrated to actual training load, fat held or slightly bumped for anti-inflammatory support.

**What is the physician's return-to-training timeline and what is the coach's contingency plan?** These are frequently different numbers. The physician says "5 to 7 days minimum." The coach says "he's fighting in 14 days, we're going to try to get him back on day 4 if he can walk." The corner needs to hold the line on the physician's timeline, but the nutrition plan has to be built for both scenarios — the on-time return at day 5 to 7 and the pushed-timeline return at day 3 to 4 that the coach may attempt. Build the plan against both and adjust on the actual return day.

## The Calorie Math During the Layoff

The most common mistake in the injury-interruption pattern is holding the pre-injury calorie budget through the layoff because "we don't want to under-fuel the healing." The physiology of that argument is wrong. Injured tissue does need adequate protein and adequate total calories for repair — but "adequate" is not the same as "the training-load budget." The training-load calories included the caloric cost of two full sessions per day. Those calories are not being spent during the layoff. Holding them anyway is caloric surplus, and the surplus lands as fat mass and water retention that will show up on the scale at the return-to-training weigh-in.

The correct math during a partial layoff:

**Cut baseline calories by the removed training-load calories, not by more.** If the fighter was running a 2200-calorie plan that included two hard sessions per day totaling roughly 800 to 1000 calories of training expenditure, and the layoff removes both sessions entirely, the new baseline is 1200 to 1400 calories per day. If the layoff allows some modified upper-body work at maybe 300 calories per session, the new baseline is 1500 to 1700. Match the calorie cut to the training-load cut, one for one, and do not compound it.

**Do NOT cut calories further to try to accelerate the cut during the layoff.** This is the panic move. The fighter sees the injury as a threat to the weight-cut schedule and tries to compensate by aggressive additional restriction. What actually happens: aggressive restriction during an injury-repair window blunts tissue repair, drives cortisol elevation that increases water retention, and produces a fighter who returns to training at day 5 with less muscle mass, more inflammatory water, and a worse cut trajectory than a calibrated hold would have produced.

**Bump protein UP, not down.** Injury repair is a protein-intensive process. Chronically immobilized muscle is anabolically resistant — the same protein feeding that would drive protein synthesis in a training fighter drives less synthesis in an injured one, so you have to load more protein to hit the same synthesis floor. Move protein from the normal 1.8 to 2.0 g/kg range up to 2.2 to 2.5 g/kg during the layoff. Distribute across four to five feedings of 30 to 40 grams each, with 2.5 to 3 grams of leucine per feeding to maximize the leucine-trigger response, and add a pre-sleep casein or Greek yogurt feed of 30 to 40 grams to hold overnight synthesis. Whole-food protein sources are preferred; [whey](https://scythene.com/products/whey-protein-vanilla?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=weight-cut-interruption-mid-camp-injury-protocol) is fine as a supplemental delivery when time or appetite is limited but not as the primary intake.

**Hold or slightly increase [omega-3](https://scythene.com/products/omega-3?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=weight-cut-interruption-mid-camp-injury-protocol) intake for the layoff window.** Fish oil at 2 to 3 grams of combined EPA/DHA per day during a soft-tissue injury is well-supported in the literature for anti-inflammatory tissue-repair benefit. If the fighter is already on fish oil in the camp plan, hold the dose. If not, this is a reasonable addition for the 5-to-7-day window, coordinated with the physician if the fighter is on any anticoagulant or NSAID protocol.

**Do NOT stack aggressive NSAID use for the layoff.** Chronic ibuprofen or naproxen at high doses blunts satellite-cell activation and impairs the muscle-repair signaling the fighter needs. Short-course NSAIDs for symptomatic relief in the first 48 hours are fine and are what the physician typically prescribes; stacking them beyond that on a daily basis for the full layoff window is a decision that trades short-term comfort for compromised tissue repair. If the fighter is uncomfortable enough at day 4 to need daily NSAIDs, that is a signal the return-to-training push at day 5 is premature.

## The Training-Load Modifications That Keep Some Conditioning

The physician-approved modifications during the layoff are as important to the nutrition plan as the removed sessions are. Working with the coach to structure the modified week:

**Upper-body strength: 2 sessions in the layoff window.** Maintaining upper-body strength stimulus prevents the systemic detraining response that a complete-rest layoff produces. These sessions burn 250 to 400 calories depending on load and duration and add that back into the daily calorie budget on their days.

**Stationary bike zone-2 conditioning if lower-body allows: 2 to 3 sessions of 30 to 45 minutes.** For a calf strain or ankle sprain that is bike-tolerable, zone-2 stationary cycling is often approved by the physician after day 2 because the load pattern is different from running. This maintains aerobic base and burns 250 to 400 calories per session.

**Grip, neck, core, and rotator-cuff work: daily.** These are almost always physician-approved regardless of the specific injury and they burn calories at a low but non-zero rate. They also maintain the training habit through the layoff, which is a meaningful piece of the psychology of returning to full training.

**Weight-cut-specific work: hold.** If the cut plan includes sauna sessions in the last week, those are not affected by most soft-tissue injuries and can hold on schedule with physician approval. If it includes hot-water baths, similar. The water-cut is largely independent of the training-load cut and can proceed as planned pending the return-to-training decision.

## The Return-to-Training Reload

Day 5 to 7 arrives, the physician clears full-training with monitoring, and the fighter is back on the mat. This is the second-highest-risk moment in the whole protocol — the day where the calorie budget has to move back up and the training load has to ramp back up without a full-strength first session that reproduces the injury.

The reload rules:

**Do NOT try to make up the volume in the first session back.** The instinct at day 6 is to run a hard sparring session to prove the fighter is fully back and to check the fight-week readiness. This is the session that most commonly re-injures the same tissue. First session back is 60 to 70 percent of pre-injury intensity, half the pre-injury volume, and coach-monitored for return-of-symptoms.

**Ramp calories back up over 3 to 4 days matched to the training-load ramp.** If the reload week is 60-70-85-100 percent volume across four sessions, the calorie plan is +200-400-600-800 calories per day above the layoff baseline, respectively. Do not jump straight back to the pre-injury calorie plan on day 1 of the reload — that is a caloric surplus for a partial training day and lands on the scale.

**Watch the scale for the first-morning weigh-in on days 1 through 3 of the reload but do NOT react.** The first-morning weigh-ins on the reload days are noisy. Glycogen is refilling, water is returning with the glycogen at 3 grams water per gram glycogen, and the scale will move up 2 to 4 pounds in the first 72 hours of the reload without any actual fat gain. This is expected, this is the fuel-tank refilling, and it is not a data point that should drive a panic cut. Trust the trajectory. The 5-day-out weigh-in is the number that should matter for cut-plan decisions.

**Reassess the cut trajectory at day 3 of the reload against the fight-week plan.** By this point the injury-related fluid shift has resolved, glycogen has largely refilled, and the scale is giving usable data again. Look at the number against the day-14-out target and the day-7-out target from the original plan. If the trajectory is within 1.5 to 2 pounds of the planned trajectory, the plan holds. If it is off by more than 2 pounds, the last week of camp needs a modified cut plan — typically an extra sauna session, an extra sodium taper, or a small additional carb reduction in the last 48 hours. Do not compress the entire correction into the last 24 hours — that is the pattern that produces the missed weigh-in or the emergency second-attempt cut.

## The Four Decisions the Coach Needs to Make Before Return

Before the fighter walks back into full training, the coach and the corner have to agree on four decisions and communicate them to the fighter in writing:

**Decision 1: Is the fight still on?** For a 5-to-7-day layoff on a soft-tissue injury that clears the physician exam at day 6, the answer is usually yes. For a 10-day layoff or an injury that is symptomatic at day 6, the answer may be no, and pulling out of the fight at day 8-to-10-out is a more responsible decision than fighting on a compromised tissue that will re-injure in the first round. That conversation has to happen at the return-to-training decision, not later.

**Decision 2: Is the weight class still on?** If the layoff has moved the trajectory more than 4 pounds off the plan, a same-day negotiation with the promoter for a catch-weight or a next-weight-class fight may be a better option than fighting depleted after an aggressive last-week cut to make the original weight. This is a hard conversation and it is the coach's call, but the RD and the corner should have the trajectory data ready to support the discussion.

**Decision 3: What is the sparring modification for the return week?** Full sparring at day 6 is rarely the right call even with physician clearance. Modified sparring (technical, low-intensity, no head-hunting) at day 6, moving to normal sparring at day 8, is the pattern I see with the best return-to-full-camp outcomes. Get this scheduled and communicate it to the sparring partners so they are calibrating their own intensity accordingly.

**Decision 4: What is the fight-week weight-cut modification against the original plan?** Rebuild the last-week cut plan against the actual day-of-reload weight and the actual physician clearance level. Do not run the original last-week plan by default — the original plan assumed a fighter who had trained straight through the previous week, and the actual fighter on day-7-out is a different starting condition.

## What This Protocol Prevents

The mid-camp injury interruption is one of the most common camp-derailing events in combat sports and one of the least-often-planned-for. Camps that do not have a protocol either lose the cut, lose the fighter to under-fueling, or lose the fight to under-preparation from a compressed return-to-training week. The protocol above does not fix the injury — that is on the physician and the physical therapist — but it fixes the everything-around-the-injury piece that most camps improvise poorly.

The single biggest lesson from watching this pattern repeatedly: the day of the injury is when the plan gets made, not day 3, and not the return-to-training day. If the plan gets made in the first 24 hours with the physician's opinion in hand and the coach's contingency in hand, the layoff week runs on rails and the return-to-training week has a chance. If the plan gets made day-by-day as things happen, the fighter is compensating on adrenaline and instinct through a period where their body is asking for very specific and very unglamorous inputs. The camp that runs the protocol is the camp that gets the fighter to weigh-in on weight and to fight night sharp. The camp that improvises is the camp where the injury week costs the fight.

Every camp needs an injury interruption protocol on the shelf before it is needed, not written under pressure the day the fighter limps off the mat.
