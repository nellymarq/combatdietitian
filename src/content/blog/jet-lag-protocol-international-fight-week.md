A regional pro signs the biggest contract of his career Tuesday morning. The fight is in Abu Dhabi on a Saturday 23 days out. He is in Phoenix. The venue is 11 time zones ahead of camp. He has fought internationally once before — a short-notice trip to London 18 months ago where he landed Thursday, weighed in Friday morning, fought Saturday night, and lost a split decision he spent six months reviewing on tape. Watching that fight back, he could not stop seeing the half-step he was behind on every exchange and the way his gas tank fell off a cliff in the back half of round two. The weight cut was clean, the camp was clean, the diet was clean. The schedule was the problem. He flew in late, ate like he was still at home, slept when the hotel was quiet rather than when his body wanted, and fought a man whose circadian clock was set to Greenwich time while his was set to the Mountain time zone.

Most international-fight losses that are not a straight talent gap are a clock problem. The fighter who crosses 6 to 10 time zones and does nothing to actively reset the clock arrives in a de-tuned body: reaction time slowed by 50 to 100 ms, grip strength down 5 to 8 percent in the first 72 hours in-country, peak power on a walkout jump test down 8 to 12 percent, gastric emptying running on home time against a venue-time weigh-in meal, cortisol peaking at the wrong hour, and the hormonal environment of the fight ring not matching the environment of the body under the lights. The talent gap on paper is zero. The talent gap on fight night is three circadian hours and a slow left hand.

This is the ground-truth jet lag protocol for the combat athlete flying 3 or more time zones to compete. The reset uses four coordinated levers — light exposure as the dominant zeitgeber, exogenous melatonin as the pharmacological pull toward the destination night, meal timing as the second zeitgeber, and strategic caffeine as the alertness pacing tool — along the 7-day window that bridges the last training session at home and the walkout at the venue. The protocol is cheap, mechanical, repeatable, and sits inside the normal fight-week nutrition and training structure without requiring any new equipment. The fighter and the coach rehearse it in camp, print it, and execute it the same way they execute the weight cut.

## What Jet Lag Actually Is

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus runs a free-running circadian clock on an approximately 24.2-hour cycle in the adult human. The clock is entrained to the 24-hour day-night cycle by zeitgebers — "time-givers" — of which the dominant one is retinal light exposure, with meal timing, exercise timing, and ambient-temperature-cycle timing as secondary pulls. When the fighter crosses time zones faster than the clock can shift, the SCN continues running on home time against a shifted light-dark cycle, meal schedule, and training schedule — and every downstream physiology output that the SCN drives (cortisol release, melatonin release, core body temperature cycle, hepatic gluconeogenesis timing, gastric emptying, bowel motility, muscle protein synthesis, cognitive alertness) runs on home time against venue demands.

The clock does not snap. Without active intervention, the circadian system resets at approximately 1 hour per day for an eastward trip and 1.5 hours per day for a westward trip. An eastbound flight across 8 time zones leaves the fighter running an 8-hour phase-advanced demand against a clock that will passively close the gap at 1 hour per day — which is Day 8 of the trip, long after the fight. A westbound flight across 8 time zones leaves the fighter running an 8-hour phase-delayed demand against a clock that will passively close the gap at 1.5 hours per day — Day 5 or 6 of the trip, still past the usual fight window. The active-intervention protocol closes the same gap at 1.5 to 2 hours per day eastward and 2 to 2.5 hours per day westward, which is the difference between arriving at the venue with a fully resynchronized clock and arriving half-tuned.

The clinical consequence of an unresynchronized clock on fight day is that peak alertness, peak reaction time, peak grip strength, and peak anaerobic power all sit on their home-time trajectory. A fighter walking out at 10 p.m. venue time for a fight that corresponds to his home 7 a.m. is swinging from a body whose cortisol is on its morning rise, whose melatonin is in late suppression, whose core body temperature is close to the daily minimum, and whose neuromuscular alertness is pre-breakfast. The man he is fighting, who lives in-country or who has executed a reset protocol, is at the daily peak of all four variables simultaneously. The ability gap on fight night is manufactured by the clock, not the talent.

## The Four Zeitgebers and the Direction Rule

**Light exposure is the dominant zeitgeber.** The timing of bright light hitting the retina pulls the SCN toward the time zone the fighter is trying to synchronize to. Morning light advances the clock (shifts the body earlier, pulls toward an eastward destination); evening light delays the clock (shifts the body later, pulls toward a westward destination). The transition point is the core body temperature minimum, which sits approximately 2 hours before the usual waking time. Light exposure before the temperature minimum delays the clock, light exposure after the temperature minimum advances the clock, and the light-timing protocol therefore depends on where the fighter is in their current circadian phase.

**Exogenous melatonin is the pharmacological pull.** Melatonin at 0.3 to 0.5 mg is a phase-shifting agent that pulls the SCN toward the time of administration being treated as "night." Administered in the evening of the destination time zone (even while the fighter is still physically at home), melatonin advances the clock toward the destination time. Administered in the morning of the destination time zone, melatonin delays the clock toward a westward shift. The dose that moves the phase marker is low — 0.3 to 0.5 mg is the pharmacologically relevant range for phase-shifting, not the 3 to 10 mg sedative dose most retail products carry.

**Meal timing is the second zeitgeber.** The SCN runs the central clock, but every peripheral tissue (liver, gut, muscle, adipose) carries its own clock entrained primarily by meal timing. The fighter who eats breakfast at destination time rather than home time pulls the hepatic and gut clocks toward the destination phase, which closes the SCN-peripheral desynchrony that produces most of the gastrointestinal and energy-availability symptoms of jet lag. A single large meal at the destination-time breakfast window, followed by a destination-time overnight fast of 12 to 14 hours, is one of the strongest non-light interventions for pulling the peripheral clocks forward or back.

**Caffeine is the alertness pacing tool, not a zeitgeber.** Caffeine does not shift the SCN at normal doses. It suppresses the perceived fatigue of being awake during the biological-night phase, which lets the fighter stay functional through the destination day while the clock is still catching up. The strategic use is timing caffeine against the destination-time waking window and withholding it from the destination-time evening window to protect the night-time melatonin signal and the sleep onset.

**The direction rule.** Eastward travel is the harder reset because the circadian system resists phase-advances more than phase-delays. The protocol is more aggressive eastward (earlier bedtime pre-shifts, pre-trip morning light, destination-evening melatonin) and more forgiving westward (later bedtime pre-shifts, destination-morning light, destination-morning melatonin). Westward travel across 3 to 5 time zones often resynchronizes passively within 48 to 72 hours and may need no formal protocol beyond meal-timing discipline; eastward travel across any clinically meaningful distance earns the full protocol.

## The 7-Day Pre-Flight-Through-Weigh-In Timeline (Eastward, 8-Time-Zone Example)

Reference scenario: The fighter is in the US Mountain time zone. The venue is 8 time zones east (e.g., Abu Dhabi, +11 hours relative to Mountain; or Berlin, +7 to +8 depending on daylight time). Fight is Saturday night venue time. Flight departs the previous Monday. The 7-day window is Day -7 (Sunday) through Day 0 (Saturday fight). Scale the aggressiveness of the pre-trip shifts to the number of time zones — 3 zones gets 1-hour shifts, 5 to 6 zones gets 90-minute shifts, 8+ zones gets 2-hour shifts.

**Day -7 (Sunday, 7 days out, pre-flight).** Pre-trip phase-advance begins. Bedtime moves 1 hour earlier than normal (10 p.m. instead of 11 p.m. home time). Wake time moves 1 hour earlier (6 a.m. instead of 7 a.m. home time). Breakfast moves 1 hour earlier. Dinner moves 1 hour earlier. Morning light exposure for 30 to 45 minutes immediately on waking — outside, no sunglasses, direct outdoor light; indoor 10,000-lux light-therapy lamp is a defensible fallback for a dawn hour or a cloudy climate. 0.5 mg melatonin at the new 10 p.m. bedtime. Daily [magnesium bisglycinate](https://scythene.com/supplements/magnesium-glycinate?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=jet-lag-protocol-international-fight-week) at 275 mg elemental magnesium with the evening meal to support the magnesium-melatonin-GABAergic axis across the shift. Training session timed earlier in the day to match the new wake-shift pattern.

**Day -6 (Monday, flight day).** Bedtime shifts another hour earlier (9 p.m.). Wake 5 a.m. Breakfast and training earlier. Light exposure 30 to 45 minutes on waking. Afternoon flight to the East Coast or international hub. If flying to the venue direct, strict discipline on in-flight routine: the moment the plane doors close, set the watch to destination time, eat at destination-time meal windows, drink electrolytes at destination-time intervals, nap only during destination-time overnight hours (if the overnight is during the flight; otherwise stay awake and use seat-stretches, hydration, and sunlight-through-the-window cues to stay oriented). Avoid in-flight alcohol — a single drink costs 3 to 4 hours of clean sleep architecture on top of the circadian hit. Avoid heavy in-flight meals that are not consistent with the destination-time schedule. Melatonin 0.5 mg at the destination-time bedtime (which may be mid-flight) if the overnight sleep window is in the air.

**Day -5 (Tuesday, in-country arrival or Day 2 of pre-shift).** Arrive at the venue by Tuesday if the flight is a 15-to-20-hour routing. If still at home for a Thursday departure, continue the pre-shift at 8 p.m. bedtime, 4 a.m. wake. The in-country routine on arrival: outdoor light 30 to 60 minutes within the first 2 hours of destination-time waking, destination-time breakfast carrying 60 to 90 g carbohydrate and 30 to 40 g protein to pull the peripheral clocks forward, light morning training (shadow boxing, mobility, 30-minute walk — not a hard session), destination-time lunch with the fighter's camp-standard carb-and-protein ratio, afternoon rest with no napping past 3 p.m. destination time to protect night-time sleep onset, destination-time dinner by 7 p.m., 0.5 mg melatonin at 9 p.m. destination time, lights-out by 10 p.m. destination time.

**Day -4 (Wednesday, in-country, 3 days out).** Full destination-time schedule. Hard mitts-and-cardio session in the destination-morning window to anchor the training clock to venue time; the morning session is the single highest-leverage anchor for the circadian system because it combines light exposure, meal timing, exercise timing, and body-temperature elevation into one high-signal zeitgeber hit. Afternoon rest. Weight-cut planning session with the cornerman — review the exact Friday morning weigh-in time in venue local time, work backward to the final water-and-sodium-cut timeline. [Electrolyte intake](https://scythene.com/supplements/electrolytes?utm_source=combatdietitian&utm_medium=blog&utm_campaign=supplement-links&utm_content=jet-lag-protocol-international-fight-week) at destination-time meal windows to replace the dehydration load of the flight and the air-conditioning of the hotel; most fighters land 1 to 2 kg below their pre-flight scale weight on a flight more than 10 hours long, almost all of it water, and the replacement is the first 48 hours in-country. Melatonin 0.5 mg at 9 p.m. venue time.

**Day -3 (Thursday, 2 days out).** Last hard training day in-country, in the morning window. Technical sharpening, no cardio. Afternoon rest. Water-loading or sodium-loading protocol enters its final phase per the fighter's standard cut plan. Caffeine held to the morning window only, no caffeine after 2 p.m. venue time to protect the Thursday-night sleep window. Dinner at venue-time 7 p.m. with the standard camp carb-and-protein mix. Melatonin 0.5 mg at 9 p.m. venue time.

**Day -2 (Friday, weigh-in day).** Weigh-in sits in the morning venue-time window. The pre-weigh-in day is now operating on a cleanly resynchronized clock if the Day-7-through-Day-3 protocol was executed; the fighter's cortisol curve, body-temperature cycle, and gastric motility are all on venue time, which means the water-and-sodium cut executes against the fighter's native physiology rather than against a desynchronized clock. Post-weigh-in refueling follows the standard rehydration protocol (sodium-plus-glucose solution first, then measured solid food in small volumes). Caffeine moderate across the day. Melatonin NOT taken Friday night — the fighter is now aligned to venue time and the exogenous dose has done its job; continuing it into the fight window produces more drowsy mornings than deep nights. The sleep protocol Friday night is the standard pre-fight sleep protocol (dark room, cool temperature, no screens past 9 p.m., magnesium with dinner, pre-sleep carb-and-protein snack).

**Day -1 (Saturday, fight day).** Standard fight-day fuel and timing protocol per the camp's standard sequence. Breakfast 3 to 4 hours before the first warm-up, carbohydrate-dense, moderate protein, low fat, low fiber. Mid-afternoon snack 2 hours before warm-up, carbohydrate-forward. Pre-walkout fuel 20 to 30 minutes before the ring entry, 20 to 30 g carbohydrate, caffeine 3 to 5 mg per kg body weight if the fighter is accustomed to the dose. The circadian system is now in alignment and the fuel protocol executes against a clean clock.

## The Westward Variant

Westward travel is more forgiving because the circadian system drifts naturally toward the longer day. The protocol inverts: pre-trip bedtime moves later rather than earlier (11 p.m., then midnight, then 1 a.m. across the pre-shift window), wake time moves later, light exposure is shifted to the destination-morning window (which may still be late home-time afternoon), and melatonin dosing is given in the destination-morning window rather than the destination-evening window to produce a phase-delay rather than a phase-advance. A fighter flying from the East Coast of the US to Japan (14 time zones west, which practically is 10 time zones east) treats the trip as an eastward shift because the shorter direction of travel for the clock is the one the SCN actually resynchronizes against. The direction decision is not the ground-direction of the flight but the ground-direction of the clock shift.

The 3-to-5-time-zone westward trip (US East Coast fighter traveling to US West Coast; Western European fighter traveling to Eastern Europe) is often clean with a 3-day in-country arrival and destination-time meal and training timing alone. The fighter who lands Wednesday for a Saturday fight on a 3-hour westward shift is clinically indistinguishable from a home-field fighter by Friday evening if the meal and training clocks are run on venue time from arrival.

## The Four Protocol Mistakes That Lose the Reset

**The "I'll sleep when I land" mistake.** The fighter who assumes a hard nap on arrival will replace the clock reset ends up locked onto a 3-to-5-day-delayed resynchronization. A short nap of 20 to 30 minutes in the destination-afternoon window is defensible; a 3-to-4-hour nap after arrival destroys that night's sleep onset and sets the clock back 24 hours. The rule is to stay awake through the destination-afternoon regardless of fatigue, drive the first bedtime toward 9 to 10 p.m. venue time, and accept that the first in-country night will be shorter and lower quality than the subsequent nights.

**The "I'll just do the trip-day melatonin" mistake.** A single 3-mg melatonin on the overnight flight does not reset the clock. The dose is too high (producing the drowsy-morning-after effect that worsens the next-day cognitive and reaction-time deficit), and the single dose is pharmacologically a sedative rather than a phase-shifter. The 7-day protocol with 0.3-to-0.5 mg daily at the destination-time 9 p.m. window is the clinically supported pattern.

**The "I'll eat on home time" mistake.** The fighter who continues home-time meal timing on arrival keeps the peripheral clocks (liver, gut, muscle) desynchronized from the central clock even after the SCN has moved to venue time. The downstream consequence is persistent gastrointestinal upset through fight week — bloating, slow gastric emptying, altered bowel motility — against a weigh-in that demands clean gut performance. The destination-time meal schedule starts the moment the plane doors close on departure, not after arrival.

**The "I'll stimulant through it" mistake.** Caffeine stacking across the destination-evening window to force functionality against an unresynchronized clock produces a short-term alertness win and a long-term night-sleep catastrophe. The protocol is caffeine in the destination-morning window only, nothing after destination-time 2 p.m., and the hard constraint on fight week is to protect night-time sleep onset above every alertness consideration.

## The Camp-Level Framing

The fighter who lands in-country for the first time two days before a fight is going to lose a fight he would otherwise win because the camp did not plan the clock. The fix is not an in-country intervention. The fix is a 7-day protocol that starts in the camp gym the week before the trip, with the coach and the fighter running the destination-time bedtime shift, the destination-time meal timing, and the destination-morning training session as part of the standard camp week. The protocol is rehearsed in the final two weeks of camp — a 2-hour pre-shift test on a Monday-through-Friday block to confirm the fighter tolerates the schedule — and executed cleanly in the fight-week window.

Internationally traveled fighters routinely say "I don't get jet lag." The ones who are telling the truth are either the 1-in-20 chronotypes with an unusually flexible SCN or they are living inside a reset protocol that was rehearsed enough to feel invisible. The ones who are not telling the truth are losing a half-step on every exchange in the back half of round two and attributing it to gas-tank fatigue. The clock is the gas tank, and the gas tank is reset in the week before the flight, not the night after it.

The fight hits the way camp was built. Build the clock reset into the camp.
