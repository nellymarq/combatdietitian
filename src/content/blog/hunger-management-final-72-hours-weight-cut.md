You are three days out from weigh-in. The cut is on schedule. The scale is where it should be. Modified sparring is done. The bag is packed. And you are hungry. Not a passing craving that a distraction will handle. A gnawing, obsessive, ruining-the-cornerman-meeting kind of hungry that has you standing in front of the pantry at 11pm asking yourself if a single piece of turkey jerky really counts.

Every fighter has been there. Most fighters lose the cut here — not at the six-weeks-out mark, not at the depletion phase, not during the sauna sit. They lose it in the last 72 hours because the discipline of the previous six weeks collapses on a single 11pm decision when the hunger signal has become the only thing they can think about. The fighter who came in on-plan through camp and then blows the last two pounds of the cut in a fridge raid at 2am is a case pattern I see multiple times per year, and it is not a willpower failure. It is a physiology failure that willpower is being asked to compensate for on a diminishing return.

This post is the working framework I run with combat athletes on the last three days before weigh-in: what actually drives the hunger signal when you are already depleted, the four physiological levers you can pull to blunt the signal without adding calories, the three things that make it worse that most fighters are unknowingly doing, and the mental-discipline layer that runs on top of the physiology because even the best-managed cut still hurts at the end.

## What Actually Drives Hunger at the End of a Cut

The hunger signal in the final 72 hours of a weight cut is not the same physiology as hunger between meals in a normal training block. Four separate systems are firing at the same time, and if you understand which is which you can pull the specific lever that matters instead of white-knuckling all four.

**Ghrelin elevation from caloric deficit.** Ghrelin is the primary hunger hormone released from the stomach lining. Sustained caloric deficit — the kind you have been running for four to six weeks of a fight camp cut — drives progressive ghrelin elevation. This is not a bug in your body. It is your body doing exactly what it evolved to do when calories are restricted. Ghrelin is elevated in every fighter running a real cut, and it does not respond to willpower. It responds to stomach volume and to the passage of time after eating.

**Leptin suppression from body-fat depletion.** Leptin is the satiety hormone released from adipose tissue. As body fat drops through the cut, leptin drops with it. The satiety response you got from a 400-calorie meal at week 1 of camp is not the satiety response you get from the same meal three days out from weigh-in — because your leptin signal has been suppressed by the fat loss that put you at the target weight. This is why fighters describe the last-72-hours meals as "not registering" or "feeling like they did not even eat."

**Blood-sugar oscillation from low-carbohydrate depletion.** If you are running a carb-taper or full carb-depletion in the final phase — which most modern cut protocols include — you are on unstable blood sugar. Blood-sugar dips are experienced as hunger even when the stomach is comfortably full because the brain interprets low glucose availability as a fuel-shortage signal. This is not the same as ghrelin hunger; it hits faster, has a different qualitative feel, and responds to a small protein-and-fat snack that ghrelin hunger will not resolve.

**Dehydration miscoded as hunger.** In the water-cut phase — the last 24 to 48 hours before weigh-in when fluid intake is being controlled — the thirst signal and the hunger signal get partially confused in the brain's monitoring architecture. Fighters who describe "I know I ate, but I still feel empty" during the water-cut window are frequently reading dehydration as hunger. This is the easiest signal to test — a small measured sip of water followed by a 5-minute wait usually clarifies whether the signal was hunger or thirst.

Understanding which signal is firing is what lets you pull the right lever. Ghrelin hunger responds to stomach volume and meal timing. Leptin-suppression hunger requires a protein-and-fat load. Blood-sugar hunger needs small carbohydrate. Dehydration masquerading as hunger needs a controlled sip of water. Treating all four as the same thing — "I am hungry, I need to eat" — is the pattern that blows the cut.

## The Four Levers That Work

**Lever one: high-volume, low-calorie foods for stomach distension.** The single most effective physiological lever in the last 72 hours is stomach volume. A stomach that has been stretched by food signals satiety through mechanoreceptors independent of the ghrelin signal or the caloric content of what filled it. Cucumber, celery, romaine lettuce, iceberg lettuce, zucchini, and cabbage all deliver massive volume for negligible calories and negligible carbohydrate. A large bowl of cucumber and celery — 3 cups, maybe 40 calories — will blunt hunger for 60 to 90 minutes reliably. Fighters who report "nothing works, I am starving" in the last 72 hours have almost always not been eating enough volume. The instinct is to eat less to protect the cut; the correct instinct is to eat MORE volume of the specific foods that do not threaten the cut.

Broth is the second highest-leverage entry in this category. A cup of low-sodium beef or chicken broth at 15 calories delivers warm-liquid volume that hits the vagal satiety signal faster than solid food. Two cups of broth in the mid-afternoon window is often the difference between holding the cut and losing it.

**Lever two: protein bumped to the top of the priority stack.** Protein is the most satiating macronutrient per calorie by a substantial margin. In the last 72 hours, the protein target should be at the upper end of the fighter's normal range — 2.0 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of bodyweight, distributed across four to five feedings of 30 to 40 grams each. This is where a lot of cuts go wrong: fighters drop total calories in the final phase and drop protein proportionally, when the correct move is to drop the calories from carbohydrate and fat while holding or raising protein. A 6-ounce serving of grilled chicken breast at 180 calories delivers more sustained satiety than 400 calories of anything else on the plate.

The specific protein sources matter for satiety. Whey protein isolate is the fastest-emptying protein and provides less sustained satiety than whole-food protein. Chicken, lean beef, egg whites, and cottage cheese all provide slower gastric emptying and longer satiety than a protein shake. Save the shakes for post-training when you actually need fast delivery; use whole-food protein in the between-meal windows where satiety is the objective.

**Lever three: meal timing distributed across the day rather than concentrated.** The instinct at the end of a cut is to save calories for the evening because that is when the hunger hits hardest and the fighter wants "the big meal" to blunt it. This is exactly backwards. The physiology of ghrelin says the hunger will rebuild between meals regardless of how large the last meal was, and the fighter who saves calories for one evening meal is fasting for 8 to 10 hours before that meal — which drives ghrelin to peak levels the meal cannot fully suppress. Distributing the same total calories across five to six small feedings across the day keeps ghrelin lower across the whole day and reduces the terminal-hunger peak that ends the cut in a pantry raid.

The specific timing I run in the last 72 hours: a small breakfast within an hour of waking, a mid-morning protein-and-vegetable feed, a real lunch, a mid-afternoon broth-and-vegetable feed, a small dinner, and a final protein-and-vegetable snack 90 minutes before bed. Six touchpoints on the day, none of them large, all of them scheduled. The fighter who runs this pattern is almost never in the 11pm pantry-raid situation because the 9:30pm final feed already handled the last hunger wave.

**Lever four: sleep protection as a hunger-modulation tool.** Sleep restriction elevates ghrelin and suppresses leptin — the same two hormones you are already fighting from the caloric deficit. A single night of five hours of sleep can move next-day hunger by the equivalent of a 500-calorie deficit that was not actually there. In the last 72 hours of a cut, protecting sleep is a hunger-management tool that most fighters underweight because they view sleep as an "anywhere I can grab it" resource. Get to bed early. Set the phone down. Use a sleep mask if the hotel window is uncovered. Skip the late-evening cardio session that pushes bedtime later. The hunger you feel at 11pm the next day is partially a function of how long you slept the night before.

## The Three Things Fighters Do That Make It Worse

**Sugar-free chewing gum in bulk.** The instinct is that sugar-free gum is a free food — no calories, satisfies the "something in the mouth" urge, cannot possibly threaten the cut. The physiology of sugar-free gum in bulk is that it drives cephalic-phase insulin response (your body preparing to receive sugar based on sweet taste) and it delivers 2 to 3 grams of sugar alcohols per piece that ferment in the colon and produce gas and bloating you do not want on weigh-in morning. One or two pieces is fine. A half-pack in an afternoon is a mistake.

**Diet soda as a hunger suppressant.** Same problem as sugar-free gum, larger volume, plus the caffeine load stacks with the coffee already in the cut protocol and drives sleep problems that come back around to the hunger issue via lever four. If you must have a diet soda, one in the afternoon is acceptable; two or three is too many.

**Weighing yourself constantly in the last 72 hours.** Every fighter does this. Every fighter also knows it is counterproductive and does it anyway. The scale in the last 72 hours is telling you noise — hydration, gut content, bladder volume, sodium fluctuation — dressed up as signal. The fighter who steps on the scale six times a day in the last 72 hours is inducing anxiety that shows up as hunger through the stress-hormone pathway. Weigh once in the morning at the same time, in the same conditions, note the number, and step away from the scale until the following morning. This is a discipline lever, not a hunger lever directly — but the anxiety-to-hunger conversion in the last 72 hours is a real pathway most fighters underestimate.

## The Mental-Discipline Layer

Even the perfectly-run physiological plan still produces some real hunger in the last 72 hours. That is not a plan failure; it is what the end of a cut feels like when the plan is working. The mental piece is knowing that the hunger is finite, that it ends the moment the scale reads and rehydration starts, that this specific discomfort is the price of making weight in a sport where the alternative is missing weight and either losing the fight before it starts or having your career and your paycheck downgraded on the negotiation that follows.

I coach fighters in the last 72 hours to reframe the hunger signal as data: this signal means the cut is working. If you were not hungry, you would not be cutting. The presence of the signal is the confirmation that the physiology is doing what it is supposed to do. The signal ends when you step off the scale, not when you eat, and eating in the last 72 hours does not resolve the underlying signal — it just delays and complicates it while blowing the cut.

Have someone check in with you the last two nights. A training partner, a coach, a spouse, the manager — someone who knows the plan and can be a phone call when the 11pm pantry moment hits. Fighters do not lose cuts because they are hungry. They lose cuts because they are hungry AND alone AND tired AND at home AND unsupervised. Remove one of those variables and the cut holds.

## The Weigh-In Morning

The last 12 hours before weigh-in are not the moment to be creative. Follow the plan. The hunger you feel at 6am on weigh-in morning is 60 to 90 minutes from being over. The rehydration that begins after the scale reads is not a snack — it is a protocol, and the reason it works is because the physiology of the cut has been protected all the way through the last hunger wave. Fighters who cheat in the last 12 hours do not just add weight back that they now have to sweat out; they disrupt the electrolyte balance the rehydration protocol depends on, and they set up the post-weigh-in refuel to sit heavy in the gut on fight morning.

## The Bottom Line

Hunger in the last 72 hours of a weight cut is a physiological signal running on four simultaneous drivers, not a willpower test. Understand which driver is firing and pull the specific lever: volume foods for ghrelin hunger, distributed protein feeds for leptin-suppression hunger, small carbohydrate for blood-sugar hunger, controlled water sips for dehydration misread as hunger. Do not save calories for one late meal; distribute across six touchpoints. Protect sleep like it is a supplement. Stay away from gum in bulk, diet soda in bulk, and the scale outside of a single morning check. And have someone available for the 11pm phone call, because the last cut of your career will not be lost because you were not tough enough. It will be lost because the physiology outran the plan, and the plan is what saves the fight — not the toughness the fighter thought was going to get them through it.
